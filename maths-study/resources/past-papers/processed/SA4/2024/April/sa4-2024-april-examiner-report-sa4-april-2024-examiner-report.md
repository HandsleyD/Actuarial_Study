Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a
revision aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.

The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The
Examiners have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and
will generally base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core
Reading specifically or exclusively.

For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in
this report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.

For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at answering an examination using open
books and online. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of knowledge
and understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should not be overly dependent on
open book materials. In our experience, candidates that spend too long researching answers
in their materials will not be successful either because of time management issues or because
they do not properly answer the questions.

Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiner reports. Great caution must be
exercised in doing so because each exam question is unique. As with all professional
examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle, formula or other text book
works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order” thinking including candidates’
ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in detail, synthesise and analyse their
findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful candidates concentrate on answering
the questions asked rather than repeating their knowledge without application.


The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that
circumstances may have changed if using these reports for revision.

   A. General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

   The aim of the Pensions and other Benefits Specialist Applications subject is to instil in
   successful candidates the ability to apply knowledge of the pensions and employee
   benefit environment and the principles of actuarial practice to providers of pensions and
   employee benefits both in the United Kingdom and the rest of the world.

   This subject examines the ability of candidates to apply actuarial practice and concepts to
   potentially complex problems, integrating their analysis into a coherent whole, and
   evaluating and interpreting results to draw explicit conclusions.

   From 2019 the requirement for detailed knowledge of the UK’s legislative and regulatory
   frameworks has been moved to the UK Practice Modules (UKPM). The Specialist
   Advanced subjects will still require knowledge of the principles of the UK market and
   regulatory regimes but there has been a re-balancing to include comparison between
   different jurisdictions and expansion in non-UK-specific topics.

   Candidates are required to demonstrate their understanding of the syllabus but in
   particular demonstrate applying their knowledge and core actuarial skills to the specific
   situations that the Examiners have presented. Consistently, unsuccessful candidates
   provide answers that are not sufficiently specific to the subject matter of the question,
   reproduce core reading that does not directly relate to the question context, or focus on
   one specific point without covering a sufficient range of points to answer the question.
   This does not enable the candidates to achieve the required marks. The Examiners
   encourage future candidates to remind themselves of what they learned in the Core
   Actuarial subjects, and to use past paper questions to practice applying these skills to the
   specific scenarios tested.

   Competent candidates demonstrate this application to the question in well-structured
   solutions, making points clearly and without repetition. There is a significant incidence of
   points being repeated in slightly different ways, restricting the scope for candidates to
   score marks. Good structure enables candidates to use the latter parts of questions to
   generate ideas for answers to the early parts (or use their solutions to earlier parts of
   questions to create a structure for latter parts). Time management is important so that
   candidates give answers to all questions that are roughly proportionate to the number of
   marks available. The questions are set so that it should take approximately twice as long
   to answer a 10 mark question as a 5 mark one. Answers should therefore be similarly
   proportionate.

   In addition, candidates should carefully consider the instruction, for example, an
   instruction to list points should be answered with a list without attaching discussion.
   Similarly, a question asking for a discussion cannot be answered with a list of
   undeveloped points.

   Candidates who give well-reasoned, relevant points, not in the marking schedule, are
   awarded marks for doing so.

   B. Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination.

   The more successful candidates were those who tailored their answers to the questions
   and thought about what was being asked, rather than just writing about what they know
   on a particular subject. It was pleasing to see in this sitting a continuation of the larger
   proportion of candidates who did indeed tailor their answers appropriately with resulting
   higher marks.

   The paper also required candidates to answer some very specific questions in particular
   contexts and the higher scoring candidates were able to adapt their answers to those
   contexts. Those candidates who wrote their answers without due consideration to the
   audience or question requirements scored less well.

   Application aspects of the course are harder to score well on, although again it was
   pleasing to see an improvement in this area at this sitting. By taking a methodical
   approach to answers, step by step, candidates were able to score well.

   It is important that candidates make sure they provide a full answer to all questions and
   include an appropriate number of distinct points for the marks available.

   The importance of structure in the exams should not be underestimated because this will
   lead to much more efficient work post exams. It is more difficult to get good marks in the
   absence of a good structure because it means that logical points are more likely to be
   missed. Sometimes points are just repeated further through the answer meaning that the
   response was more likely to look of sufficient length than it really was for the marks
   available. The computer based format should make it easier to structure answers well.

   Time management is required to make sure there is enough time to answer all the
   questions.

   Breaking the question down into smaller parts helps to make sure that a suitable breadth
   of answer is supplied. It is critical that candidates check that their answers specifically
   refer to the details of the question, using all of the information in the question pre-ambles.
   It is not the intention of the examiners to include information in the questions that is not
   relevant to the answers.

Commentary:

Part (i) was generally answered well and most candidates were able to identify advantages
and disadvantages of allowing the member these options. Some candidates laboured
particular examples of the benefits and pitfalls of having the options but those candidates who
scored more highly were able to produce a wider variety of potential examples.

As for calculating the factors, most gave good descriptions of how this would be done but
often missed the fundamental principle that the benefits will be paid for a shorter or longer
period. Some also did not clearly distinguish between cost neutrality on the chosen basis and
selection of the basis itself that would determine the relative generosity of the factors
produced.

Overall, part (ii) was answered poorly because many candidates failed to take note of the
requirement to produce a simple high level response suitable for a verbal update in a meeting
and to estimate actual factors rather than say how they would be calculated. The inclusion of
formulae and detailed technical points was not appropriate to this part of the question. The
purpose here was to demonstrate understanding of the general principles and how these might
be communicated simply.

Part (iii) was generally answered well with a good variety of reasons why experience might
have been different to expectations. The candidates who showed better understanding also
challenged the premise of the original expectations and recognised that those could simply be
wrong.

Part (iv) was another part where candidates struggled and mostly this seemed to be a failure
to understand how or if the recent high inflation would affect the three member scenarios
given, what inflation would apply in the future and how inflation interacts with the factors.
The dates for when revaluation and pension increases would be applied, what inflation it was
based on and the extent of capping were crucial to understanding the impact for each
member. Many candidates also gave far too much weight in their answers to general member
communication matters at the expense of detailed commentary on the particular members.

Most candidates made a satisfactory attempt at part (v) with the obvious options of
discretionary payments/increases and the possibility of updating the factors in the light of a
higher inflation environment. Some candidates also gave examples of other options for
dealing with the economic environment and were awarded limited marks for these wider
considerations.

Commentary:

Part (i) was a relatively straightforward knowledge-based question and was answered
correspondingly well by most candidates. However, some candidates wasted time attempting
to speculate on the reasons behind the results shown in the question at the expense of giving
better answers to the questions asked which were focused on the mechanics of the AoS.

Although most candidates were able to identify good reasons in part (ii) for the outcomes for
salary and miscellaneous experience, many found it much harder to give plausible
explanations for the accrual cost and transfer out experience items. In particular for the cost
of accrual many focused on different salary rolls being experienced rather than recognising
the factors which might have caused the accrual rate paid being different to the one needed
for the AoS funding cost based on the members accruing benefits since the previous valuation.
Similarly for transfers out, many missed the implications of the reserve released by a transfer
being the funding value for that member at the previous valuation and how this might
compare to the actual transfer values paid.

Part (iii) also proved to be challenging for many candidates who looked at how volatility in
the scheme might be controlled rather than responding to the trustees' perception that large
numbers in the AoS implied that the funding level would be volatile. Those who demonstrated
that they understood this difference between reality and the trustees' misconception scored
more highly by spotting that the deficit had actually changed very little and this was probably
due to the evidence of a good matching policy for the investments.

Commentary:
Part (i) was generally answered well with candidates showing a good understanding of typical
auto enrolment requirements but some candidates chose to focus on DB schemes only and this
made it much harder to score well. Reading the rest of the question would have helped them to
score better in this part. The problems for this particular employer also seemed to be reasonably
well understood.

Many candidates struggled with part (ii) because they failed to read the question telling them
that members would get their DC service in the DB section and they could transfer any time after
achieving 2 years' service. They were therefore sidetracked by considering what service credit
would be granted in the DB section instead of considering the funding implications of the
differing costs of accrual in the two sections and the risks of allowing the members to select the
point of transfer. Others focused too much on general trustee duties.

Answers to part (iii) were generally satisfactory although the considerations of how the revised
requirements would impact the response to the company proposal were hampered by not having
read that proposal properly in the first place.

Part (iv) was answered reasonably well for the sponsor impact (provided the candidate noted
that the sponsor is in the hotel industry) but the answers for the DB and DC members were
weaker.
