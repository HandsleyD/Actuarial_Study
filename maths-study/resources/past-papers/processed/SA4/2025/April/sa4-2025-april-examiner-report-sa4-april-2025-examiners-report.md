Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a
revision aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.
The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The
Examiners have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and
will generally base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core
Reading specifically or exclusively.
For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in
this report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.
For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at a professional
qualification examination. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of
knowledge and understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should revise thoroughly
to prepare for closed-book and in-person examinations. In our experience, candidates that are
insufficiently prepared are not successful because of lack of knowledge, time management
issues and/or because they do not properly answer the questions.
Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiner reports in preparing for
exams. Great caution must be exercised in doing so because each exam question is
unique. As with all professional examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle,
formula or other text book works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order”
thinking including candidates’ ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in
detail, synthesise and analyse their findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful
candidates concentrate on answering the questions asked rather than repeating their
knowledge without application.
The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that
circumstances may have changed if using these reports for revision.

A.     General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

The aim of the Pensions and other Benefits Specialist Advanced subject is to instil in
successful candidates the ability to apply knowledge of the pensions and employee benefit
environment and the principles of actuarial practice to providers of pensions and employee
benefits both in the United Kingdom and the rest of the world.

This subject examines the ability of candidates to apply actuarial practice and concepts to
potentially complex problems, integrating their analysis into a coherent whole, and evaluating
and interpreting results to draw explicit conclusions.

From 2019 the requirement for detailed knowledge of the UK’s legislative and regulatory
frameworks has been moved to the UK Practice Modules (UKPM). The Specialist Advanced
subjects will still require knowledge of the principles of the UK market and regulatory
regimes but there has been a re-balancing to include comparison between different
jurisdictions and expansion in non-UK-specific topics.

Candidates are required to demonstrate their understanding of the syllabus but in particular to
apply their knowledge and core actuarial skills to the specific situations that the Examiners
have presented. The Examiners encourage future candidates to remind themselves of what
they learned in the Core Actuarial subjects, and to use past paper questions to practise
applying these skills to the specific scenarios tested.

Competent candidates demonstrate this application to the questions with well-structured
responses, making points clearly and without repetition. Good structure enables candidates to
use the latter parts of questions to generate ideas for answers to the early parts (or use their
responses to earlier parts of questions to create a structure for latter parts). Time management
is important so that candidates give answers to all questions that are roughly proportionate to
the number of marks available. The questions are set so that it should take approximately
twice as long to answer a 10 mark question as a 5 mark one. Answers should therefore be
similarly proportionate.

Unsuccessful candidates consistently provide responses that: are not sufficiently specific to
the subject matter of the question; reproduce core reading that does not directly relate to the
question context; or focus on a narrow aspect of the question and so do not cover a sufficient
range of points to answer the question. This does not enable the candidates to achieve the
required marks. There is a significant incidence of candidates repeating points in slightly
different ways, thereby restricting the scope to score further marks.

When answering questions, candidates should carefully consider the ‘verb’ in the instruction;
for example, an instruction to list points should be answered with a list without attaching
discussion. Similarly, a question asking for a discussion cannot be answered fully with a list
of undeveloped points.

Candidates who give well-reasoned, relevant points, not in the marking schedule, are
awarded marks for doing so.

B.     Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination

This diet of the examination reverted to a closed book setting and it was notable that
candidate responses in general were more focused on the questions than they have been in
recent diets. This contributed significantly to the higher pass rate achieved.

Previous diets have seen a gradual improvement in the proportion of candidates tailoring their
responses to the specific questions asked, and recognising the particular context of each
question, but this diet saw a step change in that improvement. The consequence of this was
that a higher proportion of candidates produced answers that demonstrated competence rather
than leaving the impression that they were uncertain as to what was relevant to the questions
asked.

Application aspects of the course are harder to score well on, although again it was pleasing
to see an improvement in this area at this sitting. By taking a methodical approach to answers,
step by step, candidates were able to score well.
It is important that candidates make sure they provide a full answer to all questions and
include an appropriate number of distinct points for the marks available.

The importance of structure in the exams should not be underestimated because this will lead
to much more efficient work post exams. It is more difficult for candidates to score well
when their responses are not structured because logical points are more likely to be missed.
Sometimes points are just repeated further through the answer meaning that the response was
more likely to look of sufficient length than it actually was for the marks available. The
computer based format should make it easier to structure answers well.

Time management is essential to make sure candidates can give meaningful responses to all
the questions. An important aspect of this is strict adherence to the question requirements. If a
question asks candidates to "List” reasons or factors then an answer that also elaborates or
discusses the items on that list will only waste time which might be better spent elsewhere.

Breaking the question down into smaller parts helps to make sure that a suitable breadth of
answer is supplied, but it is critical that candidates ensure that their answers specifically refer
to the details of the question, using all of the information in the question pre-ambles. It is not
the intention of the examiners to include information in the questions that is not relevant to
the answers.

In the solutions that follow, each question part indicates which numbered syllabus objectives
are being tested in that question part. This is included for guidance as to what is relevant to
each question part, however the question will not necessarily cover every bullet under those
numbered objectives, and for questions requiring candidates to take a holistic approach and
consider a number of different factors, other syllabus objectives may also have some
relevance.

 Commentary:
 This question is intended to cover a potential real-world situation where a DB pension scheme
 is approaching a position where liabilities can be insured on the way to buy-out and wind-up.
 Trustees often find themselves needing to understand the transition from ongoing funding to
 solvency and then consider questions of surplus allocation.

 Part (i) of this question was generally well answered with most candidates demonstrating that
 they understood the causes behind solvency liabilities being higher than funding liabilities but
 only the best candidates showed that they understood the reasons for the extra prudence.

 Responses for part (ii) often concentrated again on the general differences between solvency
 and funding without fully explaining the different impacts between member categories so they
 missed the main reason being the discount rate coupled with the different liability terms. Most
 candidates, however, identified the breakage of the salary link for active members resulting in a
 reduction in their liabilities before the increases resulting from the changed assumptions.

 In part (iii) many candidates identified a range of strategies to improve the position but these
 tended to focus on liabilities whereas the thinking on assets often appeared to be a bit
 unfocussed. Having recognised the need to de-risk the assets as the buy-in target was
 approached, many failed to recognise the need for higher returns (at least initially) to help close
 the gap and the conflict between this being more important when the employer's ability to pay
 extra contributions is reduced but it also being more difficult when the employer may not be
 able to stand the associated higher risk.

 Most candidates noted that member movements and changes in market conditions were behind

 Commentary:
 This question is another potential real-world situation but the focus here is on the impact on
 member valuations, options and experience as well as the process of selecting an insurer and
 eventually completing wind-up.

 Part (i) of this question was a standard bookwork question and many candidates achieved full
 marks.
 Many also scored well in part (ii) by providing good potential reasons for the increase in the

            SA4 ‑ Pensions and Other Benefits Specialist Advanced ‑ April 2025 - Examiners’ report



 number of transfer value quotations being sought.

 Part (iii) was poorly answered overall. Although most candidates determined what the
 member's calculation must have been, many then concluded that the use of the contribution rate
 from the last valuation meant that the value obtained was just an assessment of the
 contributions paid and therefore not an appropriate transfer value calculation for a DB scheme.
 In doing so they ignored the fact that the valuation contribution rate is an assessment of the
 value of the benefits accruing as a percentage of salary. This misinterpretation led many
 candidates to ignore the member's calculation and simply explain what the calculation should
 have been and how the member must have made incorrect assumptions for revaluation,
 discounting etc, instead of analysing the components of the contribution rate for elements that
 may have been inappropriate for a transfer value calculation. In this type of situation
 candidates are encouraged to consider the fact that key stakeholders who are not actuaries
 (such as scheme members, trustees or finance directors) may be able to present valid arguments
 and opinions even if they miss critical aspects in their rationale or approach which results in
 their overall assessment or conclusion being unsound.

 Candidates generally found a good range of advantages and disadvantages of a buy-in from the
 perspective of the different stakeholders so part (iv) was answered quite well. However, some
 candidates appeared not to understand the difference between a buy-in and a buy-out in terms
 of which risks had been removed and some attributed the benefit or the concern to the wrong
 party.

 Most candidates scored well in part (v), although some missed a key point around financial
 strength. The better candidates were able to provide greater depth, considering issues such as
 ESG and elements of the contract terms.

 Part (vi)(a) was a standard bookwork question with almost all candidates scoring full marks.
 However, part (vi)(b) was answered less well because, although most candidates recognised the
 bookwork elements with insurer factors likely to be market-related and changing frequently etc,
 they didn't identify the practical issues of expenses, selection and member circumstances. Some
 did not demonstrate a clear understanding of the impact the stronger assumptions would have
 on member outcomes because they didn't factor in the insurer's approach to retaining profit
 margins.

 In part (vii) most candidates identified changes in mortality and inflation assumptions as being
 possible causes for the change. However, only the better candidates recognised that, although
 interest rates had not moved, a yield curve could have been used or future interest expectations
 could have changed.

 There were a lot of marks available in part (viii) and many candidates were able to score well
 by considering the process steps methodically.
