Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a revision
aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.
The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The Examiners
have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and will generally
base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core Reading specifically
or exclusively.
For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in this
report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.
For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at a professional
qualification examination. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of knowledge
and understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should revise thoroughly to prepare for
closed-book and in-person examinations. In our experience, candidates who are insufficiently
prepared are not successful because of lack of knowledge, time management issues and/or because
they do not properly answer the questions.
Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiners’ reports in preparing for exams. Great
caution must be exercised in doing so because each exam question is unique. As with all
professional examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle, formula or other
textbook works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order” thinking including
candidates’ ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in detail, synthesise and
analyse their findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful candidates concentrate on
answering the questions asked rather than repeating their knowledge without application.
The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that circumstances
may have changed if using these reports for revision.

A.     General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

       1. Written communication [80 marks]

The assessment requires candidates to produce a written piece of communication that explains a
scenario typically faced by an actuary in their day-to-day work. This communication will be
aimed at a non-actuary, although the target audience’s level of financial knowledge and
understanding will vary from question to question.

The communication needs to be of a standard that would be acceptable as a first draft. It is
important that the recipient would both understand the communication and be satisfied with the
response. The marking schedules include details of the marks awarded including the necessary
content. To the extent that it makes the communication unclear or confusing for the audience,
marks may also be lost for including irrelevant content or details that candidates have
specifically been asked to exclude from their solution.


       2. Reflective questions [20 marks]

These are a set of questions designed to allow candidates to consider the approach that they took
in their communication, and justify certain decisions. For example, candidates may be asked
what information they felt was relevant for this audience, or which terms they specifically
excluded because they would constitute jargon.

Candidates are provided with some background reading a few days before the exam (the
Scenario Material) to allow them to familiarise themselves with the scenario without being under
exam conditions. Candidates are expected to read the information provided, but are not required
to do any further reading or research around the scenario.

B.     Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination

This exam asked candidates to write a briefing for the general public outlining the challenges
faced by a state pension system as a result of an aging population. As ever with CP3 it is
important to consider who the least sophisticated reader will be, and ensure that they would
follow the key points being made. When writing something for the public no prior knowledge of
any technical concepts should be assumed, including the basic details of the state pension.
However, it is equally important not to overwhelm the audience with too much information.
Enough information should be provided to put the issues into context, and adequately describe
the problems, but details that are unnecessary for this purpose should be excluded.

Where candidates struggled with this paper it was often because their paper was too technical
and detailed for the audience and would not have conveyed the main messages clearly. For
example, using terms such as ‘dependency ratio’ without explaining the concepts of
intergenerational wealth transfers from first principals would have been lost on this audience. A

Question 1

23 April 2025


        Overview of Country X state pensions, with
                    current challenges

This article has been produced by the Treasury to give the public an introduction to the state pension and
the challenges associated with state pension funding.

Introduction

Country X provides a state pension to citizens over the age of 68. This is paid for by the government from
the taxes that it collects each year. The proportion of the population over the age of 68 has been
increasing in recent years and is expected to increase further in the future. This means that the cost of
providing the state pension is rising at a faster rate than the tax that the government receives from
working people. The resulting increase in the proportion of tax being used to pay pensions is a challenge
for the government, with many other competing demands on government spending. This article will cover:

       The basic features of the current state pension
       The challenges to the system coming from an ageing population
       An alternative way that the government could fund the state pension
       Other potential ways to mitigate the challenges the government is facing


Basic features of the state pension

All citizens resident in Country X over the age of 68 are eligible for a state pension of $132.85 per week.
This amount increases each year at the discretion of the government, but in recent years has always
increased at the higher of inflation and the change in average earnings.

The state pension is currently paid out of tax income, which means that current taxpayers are funding the
payments made to current pensioners. Currently, the working age population is much larger than the
retired population, and the state pension is significantly lower than average earnings. This makes paying
for the pension from current taxation feasible, and around 19% of tax received by the government is used
to pay the state pension.

Challenges that come from an ageing population

Country X is seeing significant increases in life expectancy, and birth rates have been falling in recent
years. This means that the balance between the working population and the retired population is
changing over time, with more weighting towards the retired population. The government currently

expects this trend to continue over the next fifty years. The following chart shows a projection of the
proportions up until 2080.




In 2020 there are around 3 working age people for every one retired person. By 2080 this is expected to
halve, with only 1.5 working age people per retired person.

To ensure that state pensions can still be paid, the proportion of tax revenue used to pay for state
pensions is projected to rise from 19% to just over 40% by 2080. This is a significant increase and means
that the government will not have as much to spend on other services in Country X.

An alternative funding system

One alternative to funding the pensions from the tax paid by current workers would be for each
generation to fund their own state pension. The government could invest a proportion of the tax raised
from working people and save it to pay for their pensions in the future. This has two benefits over the
current system:

    1. The invested money would grow with investment returns. If this growth exceeds the increases to
       the state pension over the period from when the contribution is made to when the pension is paid
       this will reduce the cost of providing state pensions.
    2. Each generation would pay for their own pension, and therefore there would be no cross subsidy
       between generations. The relative sizes of the working and retired populations would therefore
       not have such an impact.


If the current working population were to fund their own state pension it is projected to cost around 18%
of the tax they pay. This compares to 19% of tax used to fund pensions in the current system.

However, a significant challenge arises from changing to this system. The government has not set aside
any funds to pay pensions for the current retired population. If taxation from the current workforce is set

               CP3 ‑ Communications Practice ‑ Core Practices - April 2025 - Examiners’ Report



aside to pay for their future pensions, rather than pay pensions for current retired population, then the
government must find alternative funding for the current pensioners. This change would effectively double
the cost of providing state pensions in the short term.

Other ways to mitigate the challenges

There are ways to address the challenges posed by the aging population in Country X within the current
pension arrangements. If we can increase the proportion of the working age population relative to the
retired population then we stabilise the proportion of tax needed to pay pensions. One way to achieve this
is to increase the retirement age. This increases the period that citizens are in work, and therefore
increases the contributions they make. It also reduces the number of years that a pension will be paid,
reducing the cost. Increasing the contributions and reducing the cost of the pension will bring down the
proportion of tax needed to pay for the state pension.

Another approach would be to increase the working population through other means. For example, if
Country X could increase immigration at working ages, it could increase the tax paid and help pay the
state pension bill. This would only work, however, if the increase in immigration was matched by an
increase in employment.

Summary

There are challenges to funding the state pension system in Country X over the coming decades.
Increasing life expectancy and falling birth-rates mean that the proportion of tax needed to pay for the
state pension will increase significantly.

Moving to a funded system, where each generation pays for their own pension, and the money is
invested between being collected as tax and paid as pension could lead to lower costs in the long term.
However, pensions for the current retired population would still need to be funded somehow.
Alternatively, increasing the retirement age, or increasing the working population through higher
immigration could also help to alleviate the challenges of an aging population.

There are no easy solutions to this issue. The government is taking these concerns seriously and
government departments are considering actions that can be taken to mitigate the challenges.

         message that there is a change that will materialise over the coming years.
        I chose to show the percentage rather than the actual population - showing the actual population
         could have given the same message, but the general total population trend would have been
         visible (i.e. the bars would have been increasing over time as well as the proportions changing).
         This would have detracted from the key message, which is that the changing proportions are
         causing the issue.
        I restricted the data to show the position now and into the future. The past is not too relevant for
         this topic, and so I wanted to show a clear trend from the current decade.
        I kept the titles and legend very simple and clear, and ensured that the chart had easy-to-read
         and simple labels on axes, to make interpreting it easier for those not used to interpreting charts.
