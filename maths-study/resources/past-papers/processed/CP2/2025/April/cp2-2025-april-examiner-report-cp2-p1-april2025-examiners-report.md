Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a
revision aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.
The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The
Examiners have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and
will generally base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core
Reading specifically or exclusively.
For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in
this report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.
For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at a professional
qualification examination. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of
knowledge and understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should revise thoroughly
to prepare for closed- book and in-person examinations. In our experience, candidates that are
insufficiently prepared are not successful because of lack of knowledge, time management
issues and/or because they do not properly answer the questions.
Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiner reports in preparing for
exams. Great caution must be exercised in doing so because each exam question is
unique. As with all professional examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle,
formula or other textbook works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order”
thinking including candidates’ ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in
detail, synthesise and analyse their findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful
candidates concentrate on answering the questions asked rather than repeating their
knowledge without application.
The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that
circumstances may have changed if using these reports for revision.

A.     General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

The aim of this subject is to ensure that the successful candidate can analyse data, develop a
model, and document the work (including maintaining an audit trail for a fellow student and
senior actuary). They should be able to analyse the methods used and outputs generated and
communicate to a senior actuary the approach, results and conclusions.

The subject is split into two papers. The first, dealt with in this report, covers the objectives:

·      analysis of data.
·      development of a model with clear documentation.

As the focus of the subject is on communication, the majority of the marks are for the
documentation and outputs generated rather than for technical modelling skills. For example,
a technical mistake is only penalised once and candidates can still earn marks for accurate
and clear communication of what was done.

Candidates who give well-reasoned points not in the marking schedule are awarded marks for
doing so.

The Examiners recommend prospective candidates attempt a number of past papers and pay
close attention at both the model solutions and marking schedule to get a better idea of the
approach to analysing data and model documentation that the examiners are looking for.

B.     Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination

Overall, across the two papers, candidates found this diet of the exam to be slightly harder
than normal. However, taking each paper in turn, the difficulty of paper 1 was slightly less
challenging than an average CP2 Paper 1, while paper 2 was slightly more challenging than
an average CP2 Paper 2.

Modelling
The examiners are pleased to see that there is a continued improved performance in the data
analysis section. Most candidates were able to ensure the data used for the modelling was fit
for purpose and made reasonable adjustments to the data. Only the stronger candidates
spotted the low sales outlier and commented on the appropriateness of it.

The calculations required were not overly complex and were generally handled well.
Candidates are reminded that credit will be awarded in the audit trail sections even when their
modelling produces unrealistic or incorrect results, provided that the audit trail clearly and
accurately describes the modelling completed. Furthermore, where an early mistake in the
modelling affects subsequent parts of the question, follow-on marks will be awarded if the
later work is consistent with the earlier error and demonstrates sound methodology.

Audit trail
Many candidates did well in the audit trail. The description of methodology, with emphasis
on both what was done and how each step was performed, was well handled by the better
prepared candidates. Reasonableness checks continue to be a weak area. Merely giving an
observation (e.g. NPV in the additional scenario has gone up) is not a reasonableness check

 Commentary
 The modelling section of the paper was reasonably simple throughout, but had enough
 complexity to allow better candidates the opportunity to differentiate themselves and to
 display a more thorough understanding of the assignment.

 Data analysis was generally well handled by most candidates. Two out of the three outliers
 (with the exception of the low sales outliers) were identified, and a variety of corrections were
 made, which were all acceptable provided a valid rationale was given. Regarding the low
 sales outlier, some candidates spotted it and either made appropriate adjustments or decided
 to leave it unchanged. Both approaches were accepted. For credit to be given, candidates
 needed to at least comment on the outlier and suggest reasons why it might be appropriate for
 inclusion.

 The majority of candidates managed the simpler elements of the calculations well, with most
 being able to calculate the annual sales of electric and petrol cars and the cumulative number
 of cars at the end of each year, after scrapping. However, a number of candidates made an
 incorrect calculation in the number of charging stations needed by using the cumulative
 number of cars figure (instead of the difference between each year to get the net new number
 of electric cars), overestimating the number of charging stations significantly as a result.

 Calculations on the NPV and shortfalls in the base scenario were handled well by candidates
 who attempted it.

 Only the stronger candidates completed the additional scenario. The examiners advise
 prospective candidates to work on their exam techniques and be disciplined with time, so they
 do not invest a disproportionate time on a particular question or section.

 Charts were generally well constructed, though weaker candidates tended not to label the
 axes or used an inappropriate chart type. There could be multiple chart types that are
 appropriate for each scenario and full credits were awarded as long as the information
 presented was clear from the chart.

 Commentary
 Auto checks other than those used for data validation were still only carried out by the
 stronger candidates. Simple checks, such as checking the total car sales equal to the total of
 electric car sales and petrol car sales, are easy to perform and would have received credit
 from the examiners. Candidates should consider how each step of the modelling can be
 checked either automatically or with a reasonableness check.

 Spreadsheet practice continues to be reasonably well handled, with only the weaker
 candidates scoring poorly here. Separating inputs out onto a parameters sheet, avoiding
 hard-coding and applying clear formatting all tended to be done well. Weaker candidates
 failed to follow the modelling in the required order. Prospective candidates are reminded that
 the order of the questions is intended to guide and help break down the modelling into more
 manageable pieces.

 Commentary
 In general, audit trails were reasonably well written with most candidates budgeting sufficient
 time to produce a complete documentation of what they have done.

 The description of methodology has steadily been improving over time, with most candidates
 being able to note both what has been done (e.g. the NPV is calculated), and how it was done
 (e.g. by multiplying the cashflows with a relevant discount rate) in the model. Candidates
 should focus on consistently describing their method throughout the audit trail. Overall, the
 methodology section was generally well written and at the level appropriate for both a fellow
 student and a senior actuary.

 As with past exams, some candidates struggled with providing assumptions that add value to
 the audit trail. Restating information provided in the background information or listing items
 not assumed to change are not assumptions that add value.

 Reasonableness checks remain elusive for all but the strongest candidates and could make a
 difference between a pass or a fail. Candidates that are able to understand what their model is
 doing, and therefore what results are reasonable (or not) and are able to communicate this
 understanding tend to do well. Few candidates scored well in this section.
