Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a revision
aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.

The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The Examiners
have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and will generally
base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core Reading specifically
or exclusively.

For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in this
report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.

For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at a professional
qualification examination. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of knowledge
and understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should revise thoroughly to prepare for
closed-book and in-person examinations. In our experience, candidates that are insufficiently
prepared are not successful because of lack of knowledge, time management issues and/or because
they do not properly answer the questions.

Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiner reports in preparing for exams. Great
caution must be exercised in doing so because each exam question is unique. As with all
professional examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle, formula or other
textbook works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order” thinking including
candidates’ ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in detail, synthesise and
analyse their findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful candidates concentrate on
answering the questions asked rather than repeating their knowledge without application.

The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that circumstances
may have changed if using these reports for revision.

A.     General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

The aim of this subject is to ensure that the successful candidate can analyse data, develop a
model, and document the work (including maintaining an audit trail for a fellow student and
senior actuary). They should be able to analyse the methods used and outputs generated and
communicate to a senior actuary the approach, results and conclusions.

The subject is split into two papers. The second, dealt with in this report, covers the objectives:

      ability to analyse the methods used and the model’s outputs.
      ability to apply and interpret the results.
      communication of the approach, results and conclusions to a senior actuary.

As the focus of the subject is on communication, the majority of the marks are for the
documentation and outputs generated rather than for technical modelling skills. For example, a
technical mistake is only penalised once and students can still earn marks for accurate and clear
communication of what was done.

Candidates who give well-reasoned points not in the marking schedule are awarded marks for
doing so.

It is recommended that prospective candidates attempt a number of past papers and look closely
at both the model solutions and the marking schedule to get a better idea of the types of
conclusions and next steps that the examiners are looking for.


B.     Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination

Overall, across the two papers, candidates found this diet of the exam to be slightly harder than
normal. However, taking each paper in turn, the difficulty of paper 1 was slightly less
challenging than an average CP2 Paper 1, while paper 2 was slightly more challenging than an
average CP2 Paper 2.

Modelling
The modelling and the construction of charts were well handled by most candidates.

A significant number of candidates missed the fact that they should update the commission
arrangement with movie distributors to reference the updated ticket prices.

The instructions in the exam for how to construct the charts, especially the chart showing the
total and average projected ticket sales, were less prescriptive than usual, and as a result,
candidates needed to consider how best to present the results. Better candidates produced charts
that told a clear story of how the projected ticket sales differed by the type of movie.

A few attentive candidates noticed that the October release of ‘Major 3’ was incorrectly labelled,
with the ‘3’ omitted. While the background information in the exam paper made it clear that
‘Major 3’ was intended, this typographical error had no bearing on the calculations required in

disadvantaged as a result.

Summary
The methodology was set out well by stronger candidates, with clear explanations covering most
of the main steps. There was a tendency for some candidates to use less detail than was expected
in the methodology, but this is still an area that was well handled generally.

Despite multiple warnings from previous Examiners’ reports, a small number of students
continued to either refer to the spreadsheet (in the style of an audit trail) or copy the audit trail
into the summary report. As we have stressed in the past, candidates that take this approach will
gain very little credit from the examiners.

Candidates' conclusions were generally disappointing and handled less well than in previous
sessions. Weaker candidates often demonstrated a limited understanding of the assignment,
tending to simply describe what was visible in the charts rather than interpreting the outputs or
communicating their implications effectively to a senior actuary. Some candidates also omitted
the chart provided within the model. While the Examiners do not always expect candidates to
include such a chart, stronger candidates recognised its relevance, incorporated it into their
summary reports, and provided insightful commentary on its significance.

Candidate’s performance in the next steps section was mixed in this sitting. Stronger candidates
demonstrated the ability to tailor their suggestions to the specific context of the scenario,
offering a broad and relevant range of ideas. In contrast, weaker candidates often defaulted to
generic “check-list” responses - such as recommending stochastic modelling - without
considering whether this was appropriate for the situation. The Examiners would like to remind
future candidates that relevance is crucial in this section and is a key way to distinguish stronger
responses from weaker ones.

Commentary:
Methodology

Most candidates presented the methodology reasonably well, with generally clear
explanations that covered the key steps. However, there was a tendency among weaker
candidates to provide less detail than expected. Despite this, the methodology section was,
overall, handled competently. Stronger candidates demonstrated the ability to adapt the
content from the audit trail to suit the intended audience, offering a concise, high-level
overview of the methodology. These candidates avoided over-reliance on formulas or direct
references to the Excel model - an approach more appropriate for the audit trail than for a
summary report. The Examiners were also pleased to note that very few candidates directly
copied and pasted the methodology from the audit trail. As highlighted in previous reports,
this practice would receive any credit from the examiners.
Conclusions
This section continues to be a common area of weakness. While most candidates were able to
identify the most obvious conclusions from the results, their responses were often brief and
superficial - focusing on what was observed rather than how or why it occurred. For example,
weaker candidates might have noted that snack sales increased under the annual pass
scenario (the what), whereas stronger candidates went further by explaining that, even with
the 10% loyalty discount, annual passholders still spent 20% more than in the original
scenario (the how). This level of interpretation and insight is what differentiates stronger
candidates in this section.
Some candidates did not include the chart provided in the model, which illustrated the number
of unique cinemagoers and the proportion purchasing snacks, segmented by the number of
movies seen annually. While it is not necessary to include every chart from the model,
candidates are expected to exercise judgement in determining whether a chart is relevant to
the scenario and contributes meaningfully to the narrative. Charts that support insightful,
value-adding conclusions can enhance the quality of the report and will be awarded credit
accordingly.
Next steps
Most candidates proposed a number of next steps; however, only the stronger candidates
tailored their suggestions to the specific scenario and clearly explained how each step would
add value. Weaker responses often relied on a generic, “scattergun” list of next steps - many
of which appeared to be lifted from previous exams and bore little relevance to the current
context. The Examiners would like to remind candidates that the use of templated or recycled
lists is easily identifiable and typically scores poorly as these points do not reflect the
situation outlined in the question.
Candidates are encouraged to reflect on the assumptions or limitations they have already
identified in their report and consider whether these could be addressed or refined through
appropriate next steps. Importantly, candidates should go beyond simply listing actions - they
should explain the rationale behind each suggestion and the benefit it would bring. This
deeper level of thinking is essential for securing maximum credit for each idea.
