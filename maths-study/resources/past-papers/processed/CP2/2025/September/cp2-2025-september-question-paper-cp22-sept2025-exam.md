Background
You are an actuarial student working in a consulting firm. One of your clients, a major car
manufacturer, has approached you for some advice. They are looking at options for how to
respond to changes in government policy regarding car taxation.

The current taxation regime for cars was set up several years ago when countries around
the world started trying to reduce their carbon dioxide emissions. Cars are taxed in line with
how much carbon dioxide they emit per kilometre under test conditions. The annual tax
rates per car under the current tax regime are as follows (where year 1 is the year the car
was purchased):

                          CO2 emitted per km       Year 1     Year 2+
                                                    ($)         ($)
                              < 50 g/km              0           0
                            50–150 g/km             300         150
                            150–200 g/km            400         200
                             > 200 g/km             600         300

Electric cars emit no carbon dioxide therefore no tax is paid on electric cars under the current
taxation regime.

The government has recently published a consultation paper regarding a change to the
taxation rates of cars. As more consumers are buying electric cars, the overall income
received by the government under the current taxation regime for cars is reducing. The
government is concerned about this as the taxation revenue from cars is used to maintain
infrastructure in the country. The government is keen to find an alternative way to tax cars
so that all cars pay equitably towards the maintenance of infrastructure.

The government has suggested determining the taxation of cars based on the weight of the
car. The rationale is that the heavier a car is (either fuel or electricity powered), the more
damage it does to the road surface, and the more resources it uses. The consultation paper
has provided the following proposed annual tax rates:

                                  Weight in kg     Tax rate ($)
                                    < 1,500            150
                                  1,500–2,500          300
                                  2,500–3,500          600
                                    > 3,500          1,200

In anticipation of complaints from the producers and owners of electric cars, the proposal
includes a discount to the price of electricity of $0.05 per kWh when charging an electric car.

Your client’s company sells six different models of car. Cars are categorised into petrol and
electric models and then by size (i.e. small, medium and large). The company has realised
that this weight-based method of calculating the tax on cars will increase the cost of owning
an electric car and it is keen to avoid reversing the trend of recent months that has seen a rise
in the sales of electric cars. The company would therefore like to investigate the costs of
owning a petrol or electric car for 5 years under both taxation regimes, and the changes that
they could make in light of the differences in taxation regimes.

The total cost of ownership for a car is to be calculated as follows:

   The present value of all costs incurred over a 5-year period (price, tax, fuel, electricity
    and servicing), calculated using a 4% p.a. discount rate.
   Taxation is payable annually in advance.
   Fuel and electricity are calculated based on an annual distance travelled of 15,000 km
    (assumed to be spread evenly over the year).
   There is no car servicing cost for the first 3 years. Car servicing costs will be incurred
    from year 4 onwards and are assumed to occur halfway through the year thereafter.

The sales department at the car manufacturer has provided:

   the details of each model of car (see parameters worksheet in the spreadsheet).
   the current costs of fuel and electricity.
   car sales figures for the last calendar year on a monthly basis.

[Note: The profit margin provided by the sales team is the profit margin implied by the
current pricing structure. If the price of the cars changes so will the profit margin as the
manufacturing costs are not changing.]

The client has asked that we calculate:

   the total cost of ownership for each of the six car models.
   based on last year’s sales figures:
    o the total revenue and profit for 1 year based on their expected sales.

The client would then like the calculations redone under the weight-based taxation regime. A

the audit trail below that covers what has been done. However, having passed their final

Next steps

The client is considering possible ways to reduce any cost increase for customers caused by
the new tax regime. The client has suggested two proposals:

Proposal 1: Reduce the price of each car by the increase in the cost of ownership implied by
the change to the tax regime for that car, thereby reducing the cost of ownership back to what
it was for that car prior to the tax regime change.

Proposal 2: Reduce the price per car by a constant adjustment factor such that the total
expected cost of ownership for all cars sold is equal to that under the current tax regime.

[Hint: Find a constant adjustment factor (A), such that:

   the new car price for each model = A × original car price for each model.
   the total cost of ownership across all models based on actual sales is the same as it was
    prior to the tax regime change.]

CP22 S2025–6
Audit trail
Objective

We have been asked to analyse the impact of a new car taxation regime on the operations of
our client, a car manufacturing firm, and help them assess how they can respond to the
taxation changes.

The spreadsheet does the following:

   calculates the total cost of ownership for customers for each of the six car models
   calculates the total expected revenue and profit for 1 year, based on last year’s sales
    figures for our client under the current tax regime (based on CO2 emissions)
   recalculates the above for the new tax regime (based on weight of cars).

Data

The client has provided monthly sales data for each model of car for the previous calendar
year. They have also provided information about each model of car to calculate the cost of
ownership:

   fuel economy and CO2 emissions (for petrol cars)
   battery capacity and range (for electric cars)
   service cost
   weight
   price and profit margin implied by this price and last year’s sales.

Details of the government’s current and new tax regimes are also available.

Data checks

The sales data has been checked by eye and appears reasonable. Sales of small cars are higher
than those for larger cars, which makes sense given the price difference. There are no obvious
outliers although there is a big increase in December for the large electric car.

The parameters provided all tie up with expectations: larger cars have worse fuel economy,
larger CO2 emissions, bigger batteries and higher weight. The profit margin for larger cars is
higher than that for smaller cars as competition is likely to be greater at the cheaper end of the
market where consumers are more price sensitive. Service cost of electric cars is less than
that of petrol cars, as expected.

Assumptions

The following assumptions have been made:

   The data provided is correct and suitable for use.
   The sales figures for the past year are suitable for calculating future impacts.
   There are no other models of cars produced.
   Fuel cost and electricity cost do not change over the period under consideration.

Calculations-Base

This worksheet calculates the cost of ownership for each model of car under the current tax
regime (i.e. tax based on CO2 emissions).

The top section of the sheet calculates the costs of ownership for petrol cars:

   Column B has the price in year 1. Price is brought in from the parameters sheet using an
    INDEX/MATCH formula that refers to a named range covering the whole area with
    information related to each model of car, a named range for the header row and a named
    range for the column (which has the car model’s name). All of the items related to the
    model of car use a similar function.
   Column C looks up the tax figure from the tax table for the car’s CO2 emission rate.
   Column D picks up the annual service cost from the parameters sheet, applying this from
    year 4 onwards, i.e. the year it comes into effect (year 4 is also set as a parameter).
   Fuel cost in column E is calculated as expected distance travelled per year multiplied by
    the fuel consumption, divided by 100 (as the fuel consumption figure is given in
    litres/100 km) and multiplied by the cost per litre of fuel.
   Column F has the Present Value (PV) of items paid at the start of each year: price and tax.
    These are discounted 1 year less than the year in column A (so the price and tax in year 1
    are not discounted).
   Column G has the PV of items paid halfway through the year, i.e. service cost and fuel.

Underneath, there are summary figures:

   Price of car – a reference to column B.
   PV of total cost of ownership for car owner – summing across columns F and G.
   Profit – multiplying the profit margin from the parameters sheet by the price of the car.
   Implied manufacturing costs – the price minus the profit.

The same calculations are repeated for the medium and the large petrol cars to the right.

Underneath, similar calculations are done for electric cars. These are the same, except that:

   Column E has the price of electricity instead of fuel cost. This is calculated as distance
    travelled divided by range multiplied by battery capacity multiplied by cost per kWh.
   Since electric cars do not have CO2 emissions, tax is zero.

All other calculations are the same.

Total profit-Base

This worksheet provides summary statistics for the six models of car under the current tax
regime.

This table calculates profit, revenue, cost of ownership and implied manufacturing costs.

Column A gives the car model names used to look up information from other worksheets.

Column B links the total sales figures for each car model for the previous year (using
INDEX/MATCH formula) from the ‘Data’ worksheet.

    [Note: The MATCH function uses TRUE as the last parameter to look up the figure that
