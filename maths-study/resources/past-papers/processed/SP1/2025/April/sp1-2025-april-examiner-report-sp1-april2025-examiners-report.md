Introduction

The Examiners’ Report is written by the Chief Examiner with the aim of helping candidates,
both those who are sitting the examination for the first time and using past papers as a
revision aid and also those who have previously failed the subject.
The Examiners are charged by Council with examining the published syllabus. The
Examiners have access to the Core Reading, which is designed to interpret the syllabus, and
will generally base questions around it but are not required to examine the content of Core
Reading specifically or exclusively.
For numerical questions the Examiners’ preferred approach to the solution is reproduced in
this report; other valid approaches are given appropriate credit. For essay-style questions,
particularly the open-ended questions in the later subjects, the report may contain more points
than the Examiners will expect from a solution that scores full marks.
For some candidates, this may be their first attempt at a professional qualification
examination. The Examiners expect all candidates to have a good level of knowledge and
understanding of the topics and therefore candidates should revise thoroughly to prepare for
closed-book and in-person examinations. In our experience, candidates that are insufficiently
prepared are not successful because of lack of knowledge, time management issues and/or
because they do not properly answer the questions.
Many candidates rely on past exam papers and examiner reports in preparing for exams.
Great caution must be exercised in doing so because each exam question is unique. As with
all professional examinations, it is insufficient to repeat points of principle, formula or other
textbook works. The examinations are designed to test “higher order” thinking including
candidates’ ability to apply their knowledge to the facts presented in detail, synthesise and
analyse their findings, and present conclusions or advice. Successful candidates concentrate
on answering the questions asked rather than repeating their knowledge without application.
The report is written based on the legislative and regulatory context pertaining to the date that
the examination was set. Candidates should take into account the possibility that
circumstances may have changed if using these reports for revision.

A.     General comments on the aims of this subject and how it is marked

The aim of the Health and Care Specialist Principles subject is to instil in successful
candidates the ability to apply, in simple situations, the principles of actuarial planning and
control needed in health and care matters on sound financial lines.

Candidates who approach the questions, especially the more substantial elements of each
question, in a methodical and detailed manner are far more likely to satisfy the examiners and
receive a pass in the subject. Candidates will gain few marks if they do not address the
question asked but merely write around the topic of the question.

The command verb in the question and the mark allocation for each question part gives an
indication of the relative length of answer and the number of points to be made to gain full
marks. The Examiners’ Report covers more points than would be expected to get full marks.
This is to reflect the fact that the examiners can give marks for a range of valid points made
by candidates.

It is often helpful to structure and use subheadings when answering longer question parts.


B.     Comments on candidate performance in this diet of the examination

Candidates found the April 2025 SP1 exam to be more challenging than usual and the Chief
Examiner has set a pass mark that appropriately reflects the difficulty level of this paper. The
Chief Examiner is satisfied that well-prepared candidates still scored well across most of the
questions.

Questions that focused on knowledge of the Syllabus and Core Reading were well answered
by those who had prepared thoroughly. However, the paper included several question parts
that required wider thinking or application of concepts from the core reading to specific
circumstances, such as question parts included 1(v), 2(i), 2(ii), 3(ii), 3(iii), 4(ii), and 5(ii).
Candidates should recognise that these are generally the questions which differentiate those
candidates with a good grasp and understanding of the subject.

It is pleasing to see many candidates providing their answers under subheadings, making it
easier to achieve greater breadth in their answers. This also helps show that they have applied
their knowledge to the specific scenarios described.

 (i)
 Tiered benefits in the context of Critical Illness products:
 Definition:
 Tiered benefits in Critical Illness (CI) insurance refer to a structure where different
 levels of payouts are offered depending on the severity of the condition diagnosed.                        [½]
 Instead of a single lump sum benefit being paid out for all qualifying conditions, a
 partial benefit may be paid for early-stage or less severe conditions,                                     [½]
 while a full benefit is reserved for more severe conditions or the later stages of the
 illness.                                                                                                   [½]
 For example, an early-stage cancer diagnosis might trigger a payout of 25% of the
 sum assured, while a more advanced cancer diagnosis might trigger the full 100%
 payout.                                                                                                    [½]
 The levels of severity (and proportions attaching) will be clearly specified in the
 policy documents using objective medical definitions.                                                      [½]
 There are often four severity levels, but some policies may have conditions with up
 to seven levels.                                                                                           [½]
 Depending on the product structure the beneficiary may be able to claim either:                            [½]
 multiple times and potentially to over 100% of the sum insured, if the partial
 benefits are supplementary.                                                                                [½]
 multiple times to a maximum of 100% of the sum insured, if the partial benefits are
 non-supplementary.                                                                                         [½]

 Commentary:
 Part (i) - This is a standard knowledge question and was generally well answered by most
 candidates. The stronger candidates managed to provide details specific to tiered benefits
 with relevant examples, including severity levels and that the product features should be
 clearly defined in the policy documents. The weaker candidates tended to focus too much on
 explaining what a generic CI product was. The weakest candidates showed a complete lack of
 knowledge about tiered benefits, further limited their opportunity to score well in parts (ii)
 and (iii). Candidates should note that in order to maximise their chance of passing this exam,
 they should understand all learning objectives of the syllabus and have studied the breadth
 and depth of topics covered in the SP1 core reading.

 Part (ii) - This question was generally well answered by most candidates. The stronger
 candidates managed to generate a good breadth of points covering more than just increasing
 sales and attracting customers. The weaker candidates tended to focus on a limited number of
 areas such as increased marketability, increased sales and increased profitability but fail to
 consider the other areas such as impacts on claim values and operational complexities. Some
 candidates simply wrote down “moral hazard” and “anti-selection” without due
 consideration for the context or demonstrating their understanding of how they actually affect
 risks. This type of exam technique will not gain any marks, particularly given the command
 verb ‘discuss’ for this question.

 Part (iii) - This question was well answered by most candidates. Candidates who structured
 and used headers to create breadth in their answers tended to score better. The stronger
 candidates managed to generate points from different perspective covering different functions
 in an insurance company such as Distribution Channels, IT, Compliance etc. There were
 many straightforward answers to this question, where easy marks could be achieved, such as
 too expensive for both insurer or policyholder, 5% being too high or too low etc. Some
 candidates focused on a narrower range of factors, repeating similar points and went into
 detail but failed to gain further marks. Note that there was roughly ½ mark for a valid point,
 and given that there was 6 marks available for this question, students needed to come up with
 a range of ideas to score well for this question.

 Part (iv) - This question was generally well answered by most candidates. Many candidates
 were able to structure their answers and achieve a balanced view between advantages and
 disadvantages. The stronger candidates were able to understand how the proposal would
 bring in added operational complexities, capital considerations and claims exposures to the
 company rather than just focusing on customer related points. The weaker candidates tended
 to answer the question from a customer's perspective, where the question clearly stated that
 the advantages and disadvantages should be discussed from the perspective of Company A.

 (i) (a)
 Perspective of the health care insurers in Country X
 Advantages
 The fixed monetary MCR is simple to understand and hence easy to comply with                              [½]
 The fixed monetary amount provides stability of capital requirements as there will
 not be the risk of unexpected significant year-on-year movements.                                         [½]
 The approach reduces the compliance costs of having strong compliance, actuarial
 and other teams to calculate detailed capital requirements.                                               [½]
 Known capital requirements and hence more predictable free assets enable the
 company to devise its investment / asset liability matching strategy more accurately.                     [½]
 Under normal circumstances, annual capital assessments should be more manageable
 than quarterly or monthly capital assessments, hence do not create an administrative
 burden on the insurers.                                                                                   [½]
 The escalations in terms of not meeting capital requirements are progressive,
 allowing the insurers time to take remedial actions, and do not immediately result in
 losing the operating license.                                                                             [½]
 The larger insurers may find the fixed MCR low and build significant free assets thus
 enabling them to take more (both investment and business) risk and increase profits.                      [½]

 They may also reduce reinsurance and retain profits that would have otherwise been
 shared with reinsurers.                                                                                   [½]
 Disadvantages
 The fixed monetary MCR may be punitive for smaller insurers as it fails to reflect
 their lower risk exposure.                                                                                [½]
 There will not be a reward for reducing the risk profile of the insurer.                                  [½]
 The reductions only consider cash and equivalents as having 0% haircuts. The
 insurers may argue that other assets (e.g. government bonds) that could be considered
 risk free in nature should also be having 0% reductions.                                                  [½]
 Reductions may be for assets that actually retain value in periods of high inflation
 hence insurers will struggle to balance capital optimisation against hedging against
 inflation.                                                                                                [½]
 Barriers of entry - the fixed MCR may be set too high preventing other potential
 investors from entering the insurance market through opening insurance companies.                         [½]
 On the other hand, a lower fixed MCR that does not create barriers of entry may have
 a limited effect on control over the financial strengths of market entrants.                              [½]
 The fixed MCR may create false comfort for the insurers thinking that the capital is
 enough but then fail to meet events that cause significant stress.                                        [½]
