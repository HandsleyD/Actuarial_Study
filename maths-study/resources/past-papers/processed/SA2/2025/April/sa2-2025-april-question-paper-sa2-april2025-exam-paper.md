1    A life insurance company with a range of products started writing conventional
     non-profit term assurance products 3 years ago. No underwriting is performed on
     these products that are sold through both the company’s direct sales force and direct
     to policyholders via the company’s website. A sufficient number of policies have
     been written such that the company’s mortality experience is credible.

     The company has observed that mortality experience on these policies has been
     heavier than that assumed in the pricing basis.

     (i)      Suggest possible reasons for this observation.                              [4]

     (ii)     Outline the investigations that the company could perform to understand its
              mortality experience better.                                                [3]

     (iii)    Suggest possible actions the company could take to improve its mortality
              experience relative to that assumed in the pricing in the future.           [4]

     A further 3 years have passed and the company is still experiencing more deaths than
     it expected. The company’s management has strengthened the valuation assumption
     for mortality under both IFRS17 and Solvency II (having made no changes
     previously) to assume more deaths.

     Under IFRS17, the product is valued under the General Measurement Model (GMM)
     and is classified as profit making and so a Contractual Service Margin (CSM) is held.
     The only other assumption change at this valuation date is an increase in the risk
     discount rate (derived using the bottom-up approach).

     (iv)     Discuss how both assumption changes would impact the reserves (present
              value of cash flows), the CSM and profit for the product, and how the impacts
              would be determined.                                                      [10]

     For all products, the risk-free rate under Solvency II has increased, but by a smaller
     amount than the change in the IFRS17 risk discount rate. Demographic assumptions
     for the other products have not changed over the period.

     (v)      Compare the movement in the IFRS17 insurance liabilities to the Solvency II
              best estimate liabilities over the period.                                [7]

              [Note: Candidates do not need to cover risk adjustment and risk margin.]

     (vi)     Discuss why the IFRS17 risk discount rate has increased by more than the
              Solvency II risk-free rate.                                               [3]

2    A new insurance company is operating in a country where the Solvency II rules apply.
     The company has no existing business and is entering the bulk annuities market. The
     company is in the process of completing its Own Risk and Solvency Assessment
     (ORSA).

     (i)       Discuss the reasons why the company should produce an ORSA prior to
               writing new bulk annuity business.                                                [5]

     The bulk annuity portfolio will consist of immediate annuities in payment or deferred
     annuities for beneficiaries who have not reached retirement age. Annuity benefits may
     include inflation-linked increases and spouse’s benefits.

     Administration of the annuities will be outsourced to a third party who will handle all
     communications with beneficiaries.

     The company will hold assets in a mixture of corporate bonds and loans, government
     bonds and cash to back the bulk annuity liabilities. The company will initially use the
     standard formula approach to calculate the Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR) and
     does not currently make use of the matching adjustment.

     The company has identified the following key short-term risks it expects to be
     exposed to in its ORSA:

           market risks related to initial purchase of assets
           mispricing risk specifically related to insurance risks
           operational risk related to business set up
           regulatory compliance risk.

     (ii)      Outline the potential risk events that could arise for each of the above risks.
                                                                                                 [4]

     (iii)     Suggest possible actions the company could take to reduce exposure to the risk
               events identified in part (ii).                                            [6]

     The ORSA includes combined scenarios of two different risk events that could have
     a materially negative impact on the company’s solvency coverage, of which the
     following three combinations are examples:

           Higher new business volumes followed by long-term improvements in longevity
            arising from a decrease in annuitant mortality.
           Administration outsourcer default followed by higher internal expenses for
            non-outsourced administration functions.
           Credit default spreads widening and inflation increasing.

     (iv)      Discuss for each of the combined scenario examples above, the expected
               impact on the company’s assets, liabilities and solvency coverage.     [11]

               [Note: For each bullet point above (combined scenarios) the two risk events
               in each combined scenario do not need to occur simultaneously.]

3    A proprietary life insurance company has the following products in force:

           Product A: A unit-linked product with a wide range of funds. The company
            stopped selling this product some years ago, but there are a number of policies
            still in force.
           Product B: A unit-linked product with a different range of funds, which was
            introduced to replace Product A and is currently being sold in significant volumes.

     The regulator has introduced new regulations requiring life insurance companies to
     consider if their products give ‘good value’ to customers. The regulations will apply
     to all policies in force. However, the assessment of ‘good value’ does not apply
     retrospectively. The regulator has not defined ‘good value’ and leaves it to companies
     to determine what ‘good value’ might mean for their customers. However, the new
     regulations specifically require life insurance companies to consider:

           investment performance.
           costs and charges.

     As part of its investigation into whether the unit-linked products represent ‘good
     value’, the company has used historic unit prices to calculate accumulated returns for
     each of the unit-linked funds under both products over the following periods to date:
     1 year, 3 years, 5 years and 10 years, for each of the unit-linked funds offered for
     Product A and Product B.

     (i)       Discuss how the company might assess if Product A represents ‘good value’
               for customers under the new regulations.                                 [6]

     Product A and Product B both include a death benefit where customers selected a
     fixed level of life cover at inception. The death benefit payable is the higher of this
     fixed cover and the bid value of the units.

     Product A customers have the option to invest in Unitised With-Profits units (‘UWP
     A units’) in the company’s with-profits fund.

     The unit price for the UWP A units increases daily at the declared rate of regular
     bonus for UWP A units, but in any case is guaranteed to increase by at least 3% p.a.
     Final bonus rates and/or market value reductions are determined by considering the
     asset share for a cohort relative to the bid value of units for the cohort. Market value
     reductions cannot be applied on death or at maturity of the policy.

     Product B policyholders have the option to invest in Unitised With-Profits units
     (‘UWP B units’) in the company’s with-profits fund.

     The unit price of the UWP B units increases daily at the declared rate of regular bonus
     for UWP B units, but in any case is guaranteed not to go down. The asset shares for
     UWP B units are determined by valuing the UWP B units using a price derived by
     accumulating the actual investment returns achieved by the assets backing the UWP B
     units less 0.9% p.a.

     The unit-linked business is written in the non-profit fund and the UWP A business
     and UWP B business is written in the with-profits fund.

     Surplus in the company’s with-profits fund is shared between policyholders and
     shareholders on a ‘90:10’ basis.

     (iv)     Discuss if the approach used to determine the asset share for UWP B units is
              appropriate.                                                               [6]

     (v)      Explain why the returns that have been calculated to demonstrate ‘good value’
              from the historic unit prices for UWP A units may not be appropriate for
              assessing the investment performance for this fund.                        [2]

     (vi)     Describe how the company might assess if the investment performance of the
              UWP A units of Product A represent ‘good value’ for customers under the
              new regulations.                                                         [5]

     (vii)    Set out how the company might assess the costs and charges of the UWP A
              units of Product A under the new ‘good value’ regulations.                [6]
