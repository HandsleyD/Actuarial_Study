## <span class="calibre16"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">Understand the Big P</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">icture</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre20"> </span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">So what the hell is Wall
Street anyway.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Thanks CNBC and CNN,
most people think of Wall Street as balding greedy men in ugly
solid-colored suits yelling at each other and throwing litter on the
floor of the New York Stock Exchange. Of course, that’s not even close.
They might as well be holograms from Disneyland’s Haunted Mansion, just
a hangover of years gone by. Or maybe the better view is that Wall
Street is a bunch of stockbrokers, calling you at dinnertime, trying to
put you into a few shares of some hot IPO. Or some sleek banker,
(mostly) guys in the gray Armani suits, blue shirts with white collars
and Hermes ties, paying themselves jillions of dollars and then jetting
off to London to close some important deal to lever up a portfolio of
mortgage backed derivatives that end up almost blowing up the world.
Closer, but not quite.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So what is it? I like to
use this line:</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"><span class="italic">From
40,000 feet, Wall Street is about providing access to capital (for a
modest fee.) </span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">When I get a blank stare
back, I try this one:</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"><span class="italic">Money
sloshes around the globe seeking its highest return and Wall Street’s
job is to help allocate that capital to where it will do the greatest
good (for a modest fee.)</span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Repeat that often enough
and except for the fee part, it almost sounds magical. Like some
altruistic utopian dream.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Hardly. Money sloshing
around used to mean gold on steamships, but now it surfs around in
nanoseconds along lightwaves. Using fast computers and fast
communications, the stock and bond markets trade every weekday and,
sometimes violently, picks the economy’s winners and losers. Actually,
it’s not the market, it’s you and me, our mutual funds and pension
plans, the collective ‘we’, that do the picking via our buying and
selling. It’s nice to be needed. You may not even realize it, but, yes, 
magically, the value of companies with great prospects go up, meaning
they can raise capital much more cheaply to hire smart programmers or
build another solar panel factory. The flip side is that the price of
companies in doing all the wrong things, think General Motors and now
Kodak, go down, starving them of capital, a punishment for screwing up
until they disappear or do something to turn themselves around. By
finding the right price and heaping capital to those it thinks deserve
it and starving those that it thinks suck wind, the stock market does
the dirty work of forcing the hiring and firing managers and
green-lighting or killing projects. Pretty cool.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">On the street level, of
course, Wall Street is a lot nastier.
</span><span class="calibre21">W</span><span class="calibre21">hen I
think of Wall Street, I think of alpha dogs generating revenue at all
cost, getting deals done, fighting for market share against all the
other firms, and then at the end of the year, the political knives come
out trying to carve up the ever growing bonus pool, though often each
other.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">\* \* \*</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So let’s go
back.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">It’s been said that a
stock exchange can only be as large as a voice can carry. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">On May 17, 1792 after
years of shouting out on the street, a group of 24 prominent brokers met
under a Buttonwood tree at what is now 68 Wall Street, and decided to
move indoors, so to speak. They created the New York Stock and Exchange
Board, copying European exchanges and
</span><span class="calibre21">“</span><span class="calibre21">pledge
ourselves to each other that we will not buy or sell from this day for
any person whatsoever, any kind of Public Stock, at least than one
quarter of one percent Commission on the Specie value and that we will
give preference to each other in our Negotiations.” This is classic
collusion </span><span class="calibre21">that, wrapped and hidden in
regulatory language, pretty much exists to this
day.</span><span class="calibre22"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Corporations used to
only be able to borrow money from banks. But smart bankers soon realized
that a high stock price meant that companies could sell to the hungry
public some more shares to raise money for expansion. The higher the
stock price, the less shares that needed to be sold and the cheaper the
cost of capital. This is how that capital allocation game worked. Unlike
a bank, you didn’t have to pay the money back. You just traded a piece
of paper (a stock certificate) in exchange for cash you could spend. But
those new owners got a commensurate share of your profits. Hence the
trading of shares.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">All good.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Investment banking
became a very lucrative business. Outside of phones, some computers and
printing an IPO prospectus or two, all of Wall Street’s costs are
people. It’s a knowledge business. They’re not buying steel like General
Motors or drilling for oil like Exxon. Instead, it was always herds of
smart people like you finding new ways for corporate America to access
markets for growth capital. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">When Kraft wanted a new
Velveeta factory, they don’t have the time or expertise to round up the
money from pension funds or individuals, let alone trade their shares,
so they pay a few percentage points to Wall Street to do it for them.
More so for the next solar cell companies, Wall Street has always lent
their reputation to get complex deals done,
</span><span class="calibre21">again, </span><span class="calibre21">for
a </span><span class="calibre21">modest
</span><span class="calibre21">fee. It almost seems
quaint.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Commissions on trading
shares used to be fixed. At 75 cents a share. Wall Street firms made a
fortune, even though the New York Stock Exchange was still a people
intensive exchange.  </span><span class="calibre21">In the days of fixed
commissions, almost everyone who worked on Wall Street (and there
weren’t that many) rode around in limos. Really.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">In 1971, Arthur Levitt,
who much later became chairman of the SEC, was New York state controller
and trustee of the state's Common Retirement Fund.  Tired of paying huge
commissions to move in and out of stocks, Levitt demanded membership in
the NYSE so he could, in effect, get a rebate on their onerous
commission rates.  The NYSE barred the doors.  So he took his problems
to Washington.  Reform was in their air after the nasty bear market of
1974.  The Security Exchange Act of 1975 did away with fixed commissions
on trading, the so-called “Big Bang,” but also mandated an electronic
National Market System linking listed and over the counter markets. 
NASDAQ had been trading over the counter stocks in a limited way, since
1971.  But the NYSE still held a monopoly on listed
shares.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">By</span><span class="calibre21">
the 1980’s, commissions were probably 6 cents a share. Today they are
often tenths or hundredths of a cent. Big Bang indeed. It changed
everything.</span><span class="calibre21"> But it also democratized the
stock market and Wall Street. Everyone could be part of the
fun.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">From that 1792
Buttonwood Agreement on Wall Street until
</span><span class="calibre21">a bit after the Big
Bang</span><span class="calibre21">, firms retained profits to build up
their capital. Partners were often cash poor but paper wealthy with huge
sums built up in firms paid out at retirement. Corporate clients were
almost exclusively Fortune 500 and investors were big banks. Not much
risk there. Back in the days of private partnerships, White Weld or
Brown Brothers or even Morgan Stanley, that was fine. They traded stocks
and made good money. They offered M&A advice and got paid
handsomely.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">During the dead years of
the mid to late 1970’s, as the nascent mutual fund industry took market
share from sclerotic bank trust departments, Wall Street needed more
capital to take positions to facilitate big block trades. But in August
of 1983, the stock market and the U.S. economy took off. So did mutual
funds. And Silicon Valley. And biotech. And a massive service economy.
More capital was needed to fund the growth of great companies Think of
all the technology companies that didn’t even exist in 1980. Funky
companies like Intel and Microsoft and Compaq were going public,
requiring roadshows to explain what they do. Bigger deal underwritings
meant bigger profits, but required huge piles of dough. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Wall Street partners had
to pay for memberships in Greenwich country clubs, so partnerships
couldn’t retain </span><span class="calibre21">as
</span><span class="calibre21">much
earnings</span><span class="calibre21"> as they needed, so

markets, and went public to raise huge buckets of capital to help their
clients. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">The last
holdout</span><span class="calibre21"> finally gave in
</span><span class="calibre21">with the Goldman Sachs
IPO</span><span class="calibre21"> in
</span><span class="calibre21">1999</span><span class="calibre21">. This
was the end of the era of private Wall Street partnerships. What a great
25 year run!</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">OK, that’s the nice
view. Here is the cynical view. At Wall and Broad, up and down Sixth
Avenue or anywhere, Wall Street is really just and has always been a
compensation scheme. Firms generate sales, and employees get half the
money. Yes, half. The rest, after expenses go to shareholders. Sweet
deal.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">How did they pull that
off? After going public to raise huge amount of capital to run their
investment banks, partners became managing directors (public companies
don’t have partners.) But a huge question loomed over this transition:
How to get paid? On the assumption that firms could make 20% operating
earnings and similar return on equity, they would set aside 50% of
revenues for compensation – it is now the norm. In other words, the
business of Wall Street earns 70% on every dollar of sales. Better than
Microsoft or Google. Even in an awful 2008, over \$100 billion in
bonuses was been set aside to be paid in December. Hence the populist
rage.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Shareholders bought into
this compromise and did well as stocks of Wall Street firms boomed. Of
course, the guys at the top of Wall Street did even better, grabbing a
disproportionate chunk of the 50% compensation pool, but everybody did
well up and down Wall Street. </span><span class="calibre21">Reminds me
of the old story that w</span><span class="calibre21">hen asked why he
makes more than President Hoover, Babe Ruth famously spoke for all Wall
Street titans, “</span><span class="calibre21">What the hell has Hoover
got to do with it? Besides, I had a better year than he

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">A very subtle change
ruined the party. The same PC and Internet technology that was sweeping
corporate America and getting rid of tellers and travel agents and
secretaries and typesetters was also invading Wall Street. No one needed
a broker anymore - you could do it all online. Traders at firms were
being replaced by electronic trading systems that were faster, cheaper
and don’t show up late for work </span><span class="calibre21">hung over
</span><span class="calibre21">after taking clients out to Smith &
Wollensky’s Steakhouse.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">The whole reason Wall
Street got into trouble in the first place is they promised 20%
operating profits to their shareholders. By 2002, Wall Street firms,
despite being flush with huge balance sheets of capital to generate
returns with, were no longer making money in their bread and butter
business of stock and bond trading, investment banking and money
management. Too much capital chased these once lucrative businesses.
Plus, that nasty transition of electronic and often automated trading
lowered profits and obsoleted traders. Good for individual stock
investors, but bad for Wall Street, which found themselves
overcapitalized. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Here’s where it gets
interesting, and a bit scary.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">The one group making
money were these weird guys creating exotic securities, derivatives,
pieces of paper backed by pools of assets, maybe airplane leases, or
home mortgages. The neat thing about derivatives is that no one but the
person who created them knows what they’re worth, so you can sell them
at huge markups. Woo-hoo. Huge departments were created and staffed up
all over Wall Street to securitize anything that moved. This is why over
half of Princeton’s graduating classes in the mid 2000’s ended up on
Wall Street. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">With the Fed forcing low
interest rates, the higher the yield of these funky new securities, the
better.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Sub-prime home
mortgages, because of higher risk (ooh, don’t say that word) had high
yields and moved to the top of the list. When not enough of these loans
could be bought from banks, firms like Bear Stearns and Lehman set up
entire loan origination subsidiaries, and in true Wall Street style,
were aggressive and quickly rose to the top of the market share tables.
If you want to know why Wall Street CEOs made so much, it wasn’t from
trading your 1000 shares of McDonalds.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Still, those profits
weren’t enough. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">What the heck do you do
with \$700 billion and still make 70% margins on revenues (with that
50-20 employee-shareholder split)? Their customers were making great
money buying Wall Street’s derivatives. Why should banks and pension
funds and hedge funds have all the fun? What a perfect use for all that
capital on their huge balance sheets and cheap financing from low
interest rates. Wall Street, en masse, just bought these high yielding
derivatives for their own account. Ate
t</span><span class="calibre21">heir own dog food, if you
will.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">This, of course, was a
major dumb shit move. The sausage maker, who knows what goes into the
sausage, never eats his own sausage.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">\*</span><span class="calibre21">
</span><span class="calibre21">\* \*</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">It was the easy trade.
Borrow at 3% and make 6%, or 8% or 10%. They liked it so much, they
levered up. Meaning instead of just borrowing a dollar for every 2
dollar of assets they own, which by the way is the 50% margin
requirement, for 2:1 leverage for you and me to buy stocks from these
firms, they borrowed 20 to 1, 30 to 1 and even 50 to 1 if they could get
away with it. And man, it was a lucrative trade. The math isn’t quite
perfect, but these “sure thing” mortgage backed securities each had net
margins of 2-2 ½%, and then they used 30:1 leverage to get to that same
70%. So why not?</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">I’ll tell you why not.
Because all of a sudden, Wall Street is no longer a business of traders
or stock brokers or investment bankers, it’s a giant hedge fund. And
they have no idea what they were doing. None. I ran a hedge fund for a
lot of years and learned rather quickly that if a trade was too good,
one that everyone was doing it, that I should absolutely turn around and
run for the hills. But no one on Wall Street did. The spreadsheets
flashed green. Risk was a four letter word best not said in polite
company.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Wall Street became hedge
fund cowboys and loved the spoils, until a tiny little downturn in
housing made everyone get out of the pool at the same time. Deleveraging
a 30 to 1 leveraged balance sheet is not easy, nor pretty when everyone
is doing it at the same time. And turning companies belly up
overnight.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Bear Stearns gone, WaMu
too, into the belly of JP Morgan. Wachovia into
</span><span class="calibre21">Wells
Fargo</span><span class="calibre21">. Fannie and Freddie are the new
U.S. Department of Mortgages and are closing their K-Street offices.
Lehman is dust in the wind. AIG in a penalty box. Merrill Lynch is a
subsidiary of Bank of America, which barely survived their purchase of
Countrywide Mortgages. They didn’t change their name to Lynch America
Countrywide. They should have.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Now we have Dodd-Frank
reform and Volker rules and Occupiers nagging the 1%ers to give back or
give it up.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">But you don’t have to
legislate lower compensation for Wall Street, it’s already in the cards.
As bank holding companies, leverage is stuck at closer to 10%. Those
trading businesses that didn’t make much money still don’t. Wall Street
is slowly going back to the future, classic investment banking which
will take a lot less people, half as many as at the peak of 2006-7. Feel
lucky to have your seat. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Deleveraging means
smaller balance sheets, a lot less revenue and much smaller compensation
pools. But not to worry. Smart people always figure out how to make
money. If they can’t at Wall Street firms, they can at hedge funds,
private equity and even venture capital. It will be flogged constantly,
but don’t count out Wall Street. Those left standing will help raise
growth capital for great companies worldwide, and figure out how to get
paid, though perhaps not in that order. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So now Wall Street
consolidates. Should you care? Not even for an instant. I spent
</span><span class="calibre21">y</span><span class="calibre21">ears on
Wall Street, competing against scores and scores of firms, always
wondering what they all really did. E.F. Hutton. Shearson, Drexel. Heck
I even worked for PaineWebber in my early days (daze?) on the Street.
All gone. And nobody misses them.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">And the new Wall Street?
There’s only one direction. It’s back to basics. Not quite back to the
old white shoes - blue blood partnerships of the past but certainly that
business model. With a lot less capital, sit on the edge of the stock
market and provide access to capital for the next set of great
companies. Take ‘em public, bank ‘em and grow with ‘em. It may not be as
exciting as the last decade, but it sure beats the real world.
</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">At the end of the day,
Wall Street is not about the names on the door, it’s about the people
inside. Those that have a nose for making money will join other firms,
or hedge funds or start their own shop. The true money makers all find
jobs elsewhere. You want to be one of those. The middle ground of worker
bees see disruption but some are eventually absorbed into the
reconstructed Wall Street. The bottom tier go work at Foot
Locker.</span></span>

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Traders</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">:</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre31"><span class="bold"> </span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">That rough and tough
history </span><span class="calibre21">for traders
</span><span class="calibre21">meant Wall Street loved to hire athletes

</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">This is, uh, discouraged

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">How do you become a good
or even great trader? By sensing what those on the other side of the

going to keep going up or just tweaking around the edges? Are they

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Traders are often

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">But even the smartest

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Bond trading is a little

### <span class="calibre29">\
<span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Bankers</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Wall Street plays an
indispensable role providing access to capital, so U.S. companies can
lay waste to competition in world markets. And all for
</span><span class="calibre21">that</span><span class="calibre21">
modest fee, (yeah right.) </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">A little history: at
first, it was white shoe, blue-blooded partnerships that ruled the
Street, providing a gateway to the wild and wooly market. When
</span><span class="calibre21">Proctor &
Gamble</span><span class="calibre21"> needed to raise \$100 million for
</span><span class="calibre21">their new Dr. Pepper flavored
toothpaste</span><span class="calibre21">, the firm of
</span><span class="calibre21">Arntwe,</span><span class="calibre21">Bornwell
and Howe made some phone calls and placed shares with a few friends for
a \$5 million fee. But that blue blood ran red in the Street when the
go-go market in the late '60's got out of hand with 30% annual volume
increases. Settling trades meant literally sending out certificates.
Clerks in back offices couldn't move paper fast enough to settle trades.
Customers refused to pay until they got their certificates. The market
would close one day a week to catch up on paper shuffling. IBM and
others solved the trade-clearing problem, but between 1969 and 1970, a
credit squeeze meant 160 partnerships on Wall Street went belly
up.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Partnerships combined
into big firms, many who went public to be able to afford mainframe
computers. When Intel went public in 1971, it took more than a few phone

weird stuff back then. White shoes gave way to road shows and the hiring
of analysts to pick winners and losers from new funky industries. Bigger
wasn't just better, it was critical. Somewhere along the way, though,
these big firms lost their souls and investment banking became a drab
commodity. By the 1980s Wall Street had become its own worst nightmare:
undifferentiated men in gray flannel (Armani) suits. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Bankers rarely broke the
mold and those that did won great success. When a Fortune 50 firm
invited Wall Street firms in to give advice on a deal, all but one team
of bankers pulled all-nighters for a 200-page pitch book.
</span><span class="calibre21">If you’re an investment banking analyst
or associate, the words ‘pitch book’ are like fingernails on a
chalkboard.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Robert Greenhill, who
eventually became CEO of Smith Barney, flew in by himself on Morgan
Stanley's behalf, pulled a single piece of paper out of his suit pocket,
suggested three great ideas and left. He got the business.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Investment banking is a
relationship business. Once you have the relationship with the top
executives of a firm the CEO and CFO on down, the trick is to keep in
touch and offer ways for them to access the capital markets in clever
ways. Their employees need to sell
shares</span><span class="calibre20">?

a new division,

something unique. Then you’ll be there when a big financing takes
place.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Unlike the rest of Wall

MBA, and then come back</span><span class="calibre20"> (though usually

### <span class="calibre29">\
<span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Sales:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Stocks trade ever day.
Bonds trade every day. Derivatives trade almost every day. But news
doesn’t come out that often. Companies put out quarterly earnings
reports. Economic data comes out often, but not every day.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Wall Street employs
giant sales forces to fill in the silence. There job is to sell some
idea to clients, and hopefully get them to trade with your firm. There
is equity sales, sales traders, bond sales and all sorts of combinations
and permutations in between. Like any real life sales job, the more you
sell, the better you do. It’s also a relationship job. But since trading
commissions are tiny (and you have to share them with the actual
traders), the real payoff from sales happens with deals, especially
initial public offerings, IPOs. Commissions on IPOs are 7% (Facebook and
Google were the exception at 1%.) But anytime a company sells equity or
bonds or some fancy derivative, the sales force makes mass calls to
clients trying to get them to buy the
deal.</span><span class="calibre22"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">But you can’t just call
up with deals, hence the need for salesman to stay on top of just about
everything and keep in constant contact with portolio managers and money
managers and analysts on the buy side (and yes, get called asshole when
something goes wrong) so they have that warm and fuzzy relationship when
the investment bankers bring the deal of a century for them to sell
(they are all deals of the century!)</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">If you are in sales, you
do a lot of schmoozing. Kinda like Mad Men, lots of great lunches and
boozy dinners – Broadway shows and concerts, whatever it takes. There
are some restrictions because lots of clients expected to be schmoozed
so much they would demand great baseball tickets in exchange for doing a
certain amount of business.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">You don’t ever want to
be caught doing credit card sales, meaning that your only value is in
taking clients out and running up the tab on your or your companies
credit card. You want to be known as someone who ads value, offers
unique insight and, what the heck, let’s grab a nice lunch and that nice
bottle of wine because I’m the client and you can expense it an it’s
just Uncle Lloyd (Blankfein) or Uncle Jamie (Dimon) who are
paying.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">When IPOs, like

clients, both institutions like Fidelity and to lucky individuals. That

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Analysts:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">I could spend pages
describing analysts. That’s what I did for way too long. I wrote a book
Wall Street Meat about it. Read it if you want to know
more.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Here’s the short
version: </span><span class="calibre21">When the bull market hit, in the
summer of 1982, the pace of initial public offerings quickened.  A tech
bubble based on the PC played out in 1983 and 1984.  Analysts were
rapidly hired to follow software, CAD/CAM, biotech and a host of new
industries like biotech.  Institutions increasingly paid commissions
based on analyst coverage, so analysts were commanded to make 100 phone
calls a month.  As an analyst back then, I learned quickly that getting
ranked in the Institutional Investor magazine  polls, which is how you
got paid, was the great leverage for analysts, you helped your own firms
overall ranking, but other firms who wanted to move up in the rankings
then courted you.  I once was really impressed when I met the \#3 II
coal analyst, only later to find out that there were only 3 coal
analysts. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">In addition, the higher
you ranked in II, the easier it is for your firm to win IPO pitches,
what is known as the Bake Off or Beauty Contest.  And then, in a strange
circle, the better companies your firm did banking business with, the
higher you would get ranked in II.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Make phone calls, get
ranked, do deals.  Who has time to analyze?</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Analysts naturally turn
themselves into self-promoting machines.  Their incentive is a once a
year bonus.  The formula was simple:  Phone calls = Votes = II Ranking =
Investment Banking Business = Big Bonus.  Nowhere are the words “stock
picking.”  Eventually CNBC and magazine covers replaced phone
calls.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Analysts have lots of
clients: institutions, bankers, salesmen, traders, venture capitals; the
general public always ranks last on their list.  </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Unfortunately, analysts
are as obsolete as ticker tape machines, as money managers do their own
work and the public will have to as well.  </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">With democratization of
financial markets, we are all analysts now.  Everyone must take charge
of their own analysis. This is disturbing to many, but it was
inevitable.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20"><span class="bold1"><span class="italic"> </span></span></span></span>

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Brokers:</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold"> </span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Speaking of the public,
someone has to sell stocks and bonds to John Q. Those institutional
salesman can’t be bothered, individuals are too small and not
sophisticated enough for the crap they are selling. So Wall Street has
brokers who deal with individuals, wealth families and small
institutions, maybe a small bank in Idaho.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Of course, on line
brokerages like Fidelity and Schwab and E\*Trade took a lot of share,
disrupting the big Wall Street brokers (who were derided as Wire Houses,
a put down suggesting brokers were just reading the stock market ticker,
or tape, that came down the wire and generating business based on their
supposed access to the inner workings of Wall Street). </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Except even the brokers
who were left screwed over clients so many times, Wall Street had to
change their name to something else. I’ve heard personal client
services, individual client representative, but the latest to still is
wealth management.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">But they are
brokers.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">It’s not a bad job,
actually, because you can harness all the great stuff your firm produces
and sell it to whomever you can nail down as a client. Sure, it’s the
low end of the totem pole on Wall Street, but…the payouts can be pretty

their clients generate. Get a few good clients that do a decent amount

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Asset Management:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Most Wall Street firms
have an asset management division inside the company. Think of it as a
buyside firm inside a sellside firm. Other firms call on them just like
your salesman call on Fidelity. The division is kept pretty separate and
entirely different building usually. These divisions make decent money,
but don’t have the wild swings of stock and bond departments. They are
dull.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">It’s not a bad place to
work if you hope to set up your own money management firm or hedge fund
someday. Learn as much as you can and then skedaddle.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20"> </span></span>

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Floor Traders:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">A quick aside here.
Trading is critical for capitalism. Stock and bond and all other markets
provide price discovery, liquidity and the only real mechanism for
capital allocation. As far as stocks, when millions and billions of
shares trade every day, based on news and innuendo about future earnings
power of corporations, a company’s true value is discovered. Not always
accurately, markets are often missing information or have too much
noise, but enterprise values rise and fall and do the dirty work of
deciding who gets capital and who gets starved. Founders or early
investors may sell shares of their company to willing mutual or pension
fund buyers, thus unlocking and liquefying their risk capital to find
more productive uses. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So we need markets. But
we barely need humans and traditional exchanges anymore to implement
these markets. And certainly not inside a building as voices now carry
to the far reaches of the globe in 300 milliseconds and even that’s
considered too slow. Trading on Wall Street is just plumbing these days.
Value is added much further up the food chain. Trades take place on
servers in the great data cloud in the sky. A third of trading even
takes place on mysteriously-named Dark Pools, privately owned servers
that match institutional orders without ever revealing the size or price
of the order.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Today, speed is
everything. High frequency trading firms probe the market with a flood
of orders from fast computers sitting right next to stock exchanges. Now
they are trying to run software inside the Ethernet switches they use to
hook to the exchanges to get an even faster edge. Eventually, they will
bypass exchanges and create a virtual exchange with all the other
trading platforms and match orders themselves. Is this good or bad for
Wall Street? Faster and cheaper is always good, but it means
dislocations for those that work on exchanges. Beware, Wall Street is
not static. It is always in flux.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">In all my years working
on Wall Street, I only went to the NYSE once and I paid \$12 to enter
the visitor’s gallery looking through a glass wall, like an aquarium’s
shark tank. Those sharks are increasingly extinct.</span></span>

### <span class="calibre29"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre30"><span class="bold">Holding Tanks:</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">OK, there are lots of
other jobs on Wall Street. When the son-in-law of an ex-managing

Take long lunches. Complain to your father-in-law. Study for the

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">OK, so now you’re

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Yes, you have some task

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">How the hell are you

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">You could ask your boss,

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">How?</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Your primary task as

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Except no one, or at

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">So you kinda have to

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">If you get assigned a

management figures has extra time on their hands with nothing better to

didn’t think so.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">As a slight aside,

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Go find the kick ass

befriend them. Slowly at first. Little bits of flattery. Talk some

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Eventually, you’ll have

the really heavy question of how do I kick some ass around this place.
Done right, your mentor will become your biggest advocate, especially

nothing compared to the lessons and experiences that you suck out of
your mentor’s brain.</span></span>

</span><span class="calibre20">personality traits.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">And what can you expect

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">Pretty simple - how to

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">A friend of mine who

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre20">The first is to

## <span class="calibre16"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">B</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">e </span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">A</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold"> </span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">H</span></span></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">ero</span></span></span></span>

Wall Street Journal headlines today, or at least not be surprised by
anything and be positioned to react.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Eventually, others will
catch on. </span><span class="calibre21">Negative time will rule. 
</span><span class="calibre21">F</span><span class="calibre21">iguring
out how to excel will separate </span><span class="calibre21">you from
the
</span><span class="calibre21">losers.</span><span class="calibre21"> Of
course, when </span><span class="calibre21">markets are real time,
participants have to worry about longer-term issues, rather than some
sneaky time advantage.</span><span class="calibre21"> That then becomes
your edge. We’re not there yet but high frequency trading and the
firehose of information blasted to you and everyone means you can win by
being ahead of news, not reacting to it.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">That’s another reason to

## <span class="calibre16"><span class="calibre25"></span><span class="calibre3"><span class="calibre18"><span class="bold">HOW WALL STREET IS REALLY SUPPOSED TO WORK</span></span></span></span>

<span class="calibre21"><span class="bold1"> </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">This is a little bonus
(don’t roll your eyes) on the future of Wall Street. Cocktail party
conversation stuff. Enjoy:</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">I</span><span class="calibre21">t’s
easy to forget how Wall Street and banks and finance are really supposed
to work. Home loans and stock trading and IPOs all exist to serve the
economy, it’s ALL about the best way of allocating capital, by matching
risk with risk tolerance. The rest is just expensive
noise.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Credit crises and panics
happen. Capital gets misallocated. Markets aren’t all knowing. Banks
make bad loans. All the time. It used to happen more often, but the
Federal Reserve and the FDIC now smooth out these cycles, often pushing
problems down the road until they blow up into a full blown panic.
</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Let’s</span><span class="calibre21">
go back to basics. Economies grow via savings, taking the profits going
back to the invention of the wheel (or maybe the sale of that Eden
apple) and reinvesting it in more and more productive businesses. You
can’t invent capital by printing it - it can only come via profits and
productivity. Wealth shows up in lots of places, not only in that new
capital, but also in assets needed to create those profits, land, steam
engines, tractors and yes, even homes workers live in for a shorter
commute.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Human capital is the
most valuable form – you and I trading our time and energy and
intelligence working for the Man, and hopefully getting paid more than
it costs to live, creating personal profits and capital. Much of those
personal profits or earnings end up in banks, which give depositors an
interest payment on their capital, and some hand-waving about how “safe”
their principal is, in exchange for the ability to invest this money
elsewhere. Banks hope to generate longer term profits, above and beyond
the short term interest rate paid to you and me. Not a bad business, but
not all that great a one either.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">But going back to fixed
assets, no one wants to tie up all of their capital in them.  Or maybe
they can’t afford to buy the home or factory in the first place. So
banks found that longer term profit center of their own, lending you the
money to buy a home, or a business the money to buy equipment, in
exchange for monthly payments, a piece of the human profits you create
or a piece of business profits. A pretty fair bargain – the bank takes
on the risk of owning these fixed assets (if you fail to make payments)
in exchange for a piece of your output. Risk tolerances are matched.
Workers lower their take in exchange for ownership of a fixed asset they
don’t really own outright. In fact, it’s a societal bargain as well. You
gotta keep working. As Popeye’s pal Whimpie said, I’ll gladly pay you
Tuesday for a hamburger today, but it meant Whimpie had to work the rest
of the week to make enough money to pay off the burger if he ever wanted
another one.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Wall Street and
investment banks are just banks for business profits instead of business
assets. Companies sell shares of their future profits in exchange for
capital today. That’s what a share of a company means, you get a share
of profits. You used to get it literally, in a dividend, but now most
decent companies hold onto the profits for you on their balance sheet,
but still own the profit stream. Companies are willing to sell these
shares to accelerate their business plans, build a new factory, hire
1000 new programmers, whatever. Again, risk is matched with risk
tolerance. The buyer of the shares takes on the risk of future profits
in exchange for their hard earned capital. It’s a decent deal, hopefully
better than the short term interest rate a bank pays – a less risky
return.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Company shares trade
every day, meaning individuals or funds of individuals can raise or
lower their risk tolerance by owning shares of future profits in
different industries. Getting a little old and not feeling so risky? You
might buy shares in Kraft because everyone eats processed cheese. Maybe
you have a higher risk tolerance? You buy shares of the future profits
of Social Solar Mobility Nanoparticle Networks, hoping it becomes the
next big thing. Universities who hope to be around for centuries can go
further out the risk curve hoping for better returns. No surprise, many
chased what they thought were less risky buyout funds and got burned.
Pension funds balance stocks and bonds based on what their future payout
needs might be. Insurance companies take less risk so they can have
funds to payout after hurricanes and the like. Again, the market exists
to match risk with risk tolerance.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Stocks are valued not on
actual profits, but on the prospects for profits, adjusted for all sorts
of risk - economic risk, management risk, competition, politics, maybe
even sun spots, who knows. Stocks trade everyday and their price changes
every few nanoseconds as those prospects change, often inexplicably.
It’s a fun game. And a high stakes one.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">When a stock goes up,
the company is in effect, being offered the chance to raise capital much
cheaper. They can sell few shares and dilute fewer existing shareholders
to raise the \$1 billion for the new factory. When a stock goes down,
the market is, in effect, starving the company for capital, making it
more expensive to raise money. Management can lower costs, change
product plans, whatever it takes to please investors to lower their cost
of capital. For you economics majors, this is Adam Smith’s Invisible
Hand writ large, pushing great companies (or ones the markets thinks
their prospects are great) along, and spanking the ones it doesn’t like.
Economists are often confused by the stock market and its seemingly Wild
West way of allocating capital. Probably because most economists would
rather be Central Planners and do the job themselves.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Anyway, Wall Street
firms live to help companies sell shares to investors, they trade those
shares, they trade put and call options on those shares, they have funds
that pool investors money to own these shares, and sometimes they even
own them themselves. They do the same things for bonds, which are more
bank-like instruments, a loan that needs to be paid back in a 30 days
(commercial paper) to loans that don’t have to be paid back for 10 or 20
years.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Wall Street also
benefits every time our politicians decide to create budgets that are
larger than the governments tax revenue. Deficits drive the issuance of
government bonds, which Wall Street is happy to help line up buyers for,
as well as trade them 24 hours a day, to help the Chinese buy them to
recycle their trade dollars from all of us buying big screen
TVs.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">As I
mentioned</span><span class="calibre21">, Wall Street is a pretty
lucrative business, paying out 20% of profits to their own shareholders
and keeping 50% as compensation for their employees. Nice deal. Again,
all this in the guise of efficiently allocating society’s capital, so it
can hunt down and fund the most productive elements and starve the rest.
When it works, it works, and Wall Streeters party and live like rock
stars. When it doesn’t…yikes.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">The Glass Steagel Act
separated banks and investment banks as the scapegoated cause of the
Crash of 1929. Even before its repeal in 1999, banks and Wall Street
were already in each others face. Securitization was a fancy name for
taking loans sitting on bank balance sheets, funded with depositors
money, remember, and packaging them in a way to make them look like one
giant loan, a superbond, that rolled up all the loan payments into one.
This meant that banks could offload risk to others, again matching risk
with risk tolerance. Insurance companies loved these asset backed and
mortgage backed securities, since they paid a higher interest rate than
government bonds. Banks loved them because they got cash and could go
out and make even more loans. But in effect, what happened, was the
creation of a giant shadow banking system, one where non bank companies
were making loans. Well, not actual loans, but virtual loans, by buying
up these securitized loans.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Banks, by the way, serve
another purpose. They used to create their own money and even currency.
The Federal Reserve centralized this function in 1913, after yet another
panic. The Fed doesn’t make loans to individuals, but it does lend money
to banks, who are then allowed to leverage up their assets, yours and
mine checking accounts, by ten times, to make their own loans. The Fed
regulates the money supply, by increasing or decreasing interest rates
to banks to get them to create more money or less money, depending on if
the Fed smells inflation (too much money created) or a whiff of
deflation (too little money created). It’s an almost impossible task in
a \$14 trillion economy. Unlike the stock market which decides who gets
funded and who doesn’t on the backs of billions of daily trades, the
Federal Reserve is run by, gulp, people, with fallible econometric
models that haven’t worked since they were introduced in the 1970’s
(probably running on the same old clunky IBM machines).</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So in effect, banks
operate under another societal bargain, we allow them to create money,
under the supposed watchful eye and quasi control of the Federal
Reserve, which creates money supply, which lets the rest of us to go
about our business and grow the economy.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Unfortunately, this
shadow banking system was also creating money supply, but outside the
reach of regulators to control it.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Which ended up being a
problem. Because it is so easy to misallocate capital. Investors are
human and prone to spasms of momo-isms. Momentum investing. I’ll invest
in Pets.com because dotcom stocks only go up (because there wasn’t much
liquidity and every growth fund piled in). I’ll make take on another
sub-prime loan derivative in my portfolio because home prices can’t go
down, (because the Fed is so accommodative with low interest rates and
this whole housing finance monstrosity is too big to fail, or so went
the thinking.) Momo-ism almost always leads to losses.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">As efficient as markets
are, there are always</span><span class="calibre21"> misallocation all
over the place. You can always find political entrepreneurs who create
attractive profits even though they are killing the economic engine.
Cable TV franchises are given (buy really) local monopolies, so they can
charge whatever they want for cable service. Comcast is a classic
example. Their cable fees are a huge tax on society and their profits
are not from productivity but from good connections. Eventually,
something catches up with them (can you spell I-N-T-E-R-N-E-T?) and
collapses their franchise. It’s happening.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Auto makers, via the
Wagner Act, were forced to allow union to set work rules (wages were
negotiated), adding huge numbers of unnecessary but well paid workers
into the mix, making the manufacture of autos extremely unproductive.
Foreign competition eventually caught up and collapsed the unproductive
system. And finance? There are plenty of regulatory rules that govern
banks. And there should be as we control that societal bargain via
Federal Reserve lending and FDIC depositors insurance. Something else?
Well, there’s Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">These two government
sponsored entities who were afforded a cheaper cost of capital to invest
in home loans and mortgage backed securities, under the spectre of
affordable housing. Since these GSE got lower cost of capital, they got
a huge jump on packaging home loans in the securitization game. A bit
unfair, but oh well. No, it’s the unintended consequence of every other
financial institution being SHUT OUT of this lucrative game. Citibank
and JP Morgan and Wells Fargo didn’t have the low cost of capital that
Freddie and Fannie enjoyed. Either did Wall Street from Goldman to
Merrill, Morgan Stanley, Lehman and Bear Stearns. Fannie and Freddie
crowded them out.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Nonetheless, this shadow
banking system wanted to invest in something. Their cost of capital may
not have been as low as Fannie and Freddie, but they were allowed to
borrow almost unsatiable amounts of it. Chasing the sub-prime loan
category (since Fannie and Freddie controlled the “conforming” or prime
segment of home loans, Wall Street borrowed not at 10:1, but often at
30:1. With housing momo-ism as a rationale. It couldn’t end anyway but
ugly.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">It probably was some
cheap Chinese toy that caused yet another plant closing and layoffs
causing someone to default on their Palm Beach or was it Newport Beach
condo that finally broke the camel’s back. Every assumption of default
rates on packaged subprime loans started to ripple through Wall Street.
Packets routing through optical cable are faster
</span><span class="calibre21">than</span><span class="calibre21">
telegraph lines and it didn’t take long for everyone to head to the
exits at the same time, at any price. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Nobody modeled for this
in their risk profiles. Every one was a momo.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Let’s look back. In the
inflation raged 70’s, too much money was printed. Risk matching ran the
other way and wealth was safer in hard assets like real estate or
commodities than in dollars or equities. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">The great bull market
that started in August of 1982 started the transition from hard assets
back to financial instruments. Pretty cool, think of all the industries
from Silicon Valley to biotech to mutual funds that grew like weeds once
money flowed out of rocks into rolls. Entrepreneurs flourished. Dow 1000
to Dow 14000 meant amazing wealth creation - Gates, Buffet, Dell but
also rippling though 401K plans and home values.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">So how bizarre is it
that the end of the bull run came not from inflation returning, or so it
seems, but from an overextension of asset backed financial instruments.
The ‘70’s dressed up as a Collateralized Debt Obligation. I half expect
bell bottoms to make a comeback. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">W</span><span class="calibre21">ealth
needs to be rebuilt. But the Federal
Res</span><span class="calibre21">erve can’t just print it. That will
inevitably</span><span class="calibre21"> increase prices and inflation
and a return to the ugly ‘70’s.</span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">It has to be earned.
Profits. Human profits. Company profits. Fortunately, we have structure
the economy for just such a task. The stock market hasn’t gone away, it
still works as a way to efficiently allocate capital. Credit still
trades every day. Banks need to be recapitalized, but so what, that’s
happened every single downturn. </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">Every market disruption
brings change. Something different in the next cycle from the last. And
the other side of this financial valley? Simple. Risk gets dear,
commodities get cheap, the dollar rallies and entrepreneurs become kings
again. Wishful thinking? We’ll see.  </span></span>

<span class="calibre10"><span class="calibre21">It
</span><span class="calibre21">has to come from productivity. Old
fashioned ingenuity. Real profits, not political profits will refill the
bucket. So where is the wealth creation? It’s no secret that the U.S. is
still the most productive innovation economy, going through a much
deserved rest after its own feeding frenzy in ‘99/2000. Broadband
services, alternate energy, nanotechnology, digital medical diagnostics,
consumer electronics, social networks – the list goes on. As marginal
investment dollars begin again to chase productive business  rather than
home building or the buyout of utilities or the survival of terminal
auto assemblers, wealth starts to rebuild. Real wealth, not paper
wealth. </span></span>
