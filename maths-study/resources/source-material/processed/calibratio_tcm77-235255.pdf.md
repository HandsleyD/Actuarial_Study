       Improved Methods for Estimating International Migration
 The Use of Calibration in Estimating International In-migration to
             UK Countries and the Regions of England
This paper provides information about the use of calibration in the improved methods
for estimating international in-migrants at the UK country and regional level. It
supplements the information provided previously in the publication Geographical
Distribution of Estimates of In-migration:
http://www.statistics.gov.uk/downloads/theme_population/Geog_distn_in-migs.pdf

The new method uses geographical distributions of in-migrants obtained from the
Labour Force Survey (LFS) to improve upon the regional information available from
the International Passenger Survey (IPS). IPS information is based on respondents
intentions at the time of arrival in the country and these intentions may not be an
accurate reflection of where people settle. Mis-reporting by in-migrants of their
intended location has affected the regional distribution of international in-migrants as
measured by the IPS. Since the LFS is a household survey, it measures migration that
has already occurred and is not subject to the same problems of mis-specification of
region as data collected prospectively in the IPS.

Calibration provides a way of combining survey data with aggregate data from other
sources. Most commonly, data from an auxiliary source (usually administrative or
census data) are used to adjust survey estimates in order to make them more accurate
and reduce the variance. The method compensates for differences between the two
sources by adjusting the survey estimates so that their marginal totals match control
totals on a specified set of variables. New weights are assigned to the survey data
using an iterative procedure which minimises the size of the difference between the
original weights and the new weights subject to the specified constraints. More than
one control total can be used.

In this instance, the regional estimate of in-migrants from the IPS is constrained to the
marginal regional distribution obtained from the LFS. The control totals are thus
obtained from survey estimates (rather than administrative counts). In this case, the
calibrated estimates are unbiased, although the variance of the estimates is not
reduced and remains (on the whole) similar to those of the original IPS.

The method involves finding a new weight for each individual record in the IPS
subject to the LFS based regional controls. The LFS region totals are scaled using the
national IPS total to ensure that the total number of migrants is the same as that given
by the IPS. The individual weights in the IPS are then adjusted so that for all
individuals in a particular region, the sum of the weights matches the respective total
for the region from the scaled LFS. Full details of the linear method used to find the
new weights are given in Annex A. This method is based upon a formal statistical
framework and provides a means of calculating the variance of the calibrated
estimates.
Two sets of control totals were used in calibration to represent regional distributions
with age and sex differentials.
   1. Regional totals were obtained by applying the proportion of in-migrants in
      each region from the LFS to the national total in-migrants in the IPS. Three
      year averages of LFS data were used to produce robust distributions based on
      sufficient sample sizes.
   2. London/non-London totals by age group and sex were obtained by applying
      the respective LFS proportions (i.e. proportion of all LFS in-migrants that are
      in a given London/non-London residence, age and sex group) to the IPS
      national total for that age and sex group. As above, three year averages of LFS
      data were used.

Control totals by age and sex were used because the difference in the geographic
distributions of in-migrants between the LFS and the IPS, varies by age group. These
differences were greatest for the 15-24 age group, followed by the 25-44 age group.
Figures 1 and 2 show migrant inflows from the IPS and LFS as a proportion of the
UK inflows for the age groups concerned. This was most marked in a comparison
between London and outside London. Only limited further geographical
disaggregation was possible, owing to small numbers when the sample is split by
geography and age. The age groups used for the control totals represent a trade-off
between obtaining finer detail and maintaining an acceptable level of statistical
robustness at the level of a London/non-London split.

The calibration method was initially developed using IPS data sets for 1999, 2000,
2001 and 2002. For each year, three years averaged LFS data, 1998-2000, 1999-2001,
2000-2002 and 2001-2003 respectively, were used to obtain the control totals. For
each IPS year tested, the calibration algorithm converged successfully with no
negative or unusually large weights being produced. Comparison of the original
weights against the new weights confirmed that the new weights are similar to the
original weights and that the calibration algorithm did not produce any extreme
results.

Validation was undertaken by comparing the calibrated estimates of in-migrants with
estimates attained from the IPS and the LFS over the four year period 1999 to 2002.
Comparisons were made by age and sex. for groups of areas inside and outside
London, for the constituent countries of the UK and for government office regions in
England. Comparisons were also made against Census distributions and those
obtained from the National Health Service Central Register. The calibration was
found to reduce the number of migrants in London for key age groups and numbers
were increased for some of the other regions of England and for the other countries of
the UK, although the actual impact varies from year to year. These impacts were
discussed in the paper Geographical Distribution of Estimates of In-Migration:
http://www.statistics.gov.uk/downloads/theme_population/Geog_distn_in-migs.pdf

In re-distributing migrant numbers from London to elsewhere, the calibration method
evenly increases the figures for other areas. Small sample sizes in the IPS for age and
sex breakdowns within regions may result in an uneven impact of the new method for
such breakdowns. The volatility of these breakdowns is solely a consequence of
existing IPS sample sizes and is not a consequence of the new method.

The distribution of other variables such as citizenship and country of origin by age,
and sex groups were examined from the calibrated results. These were compared to
the corresponding tabulations obtained from the original IPS data to examine the
changes in these subgroups of migrants. A requirement of the new method is that
distributions of estimates for other variables remain virtually unchanged; the analysis
confirmed this to be the case.

Estimates of variance were calculated for the calibrated estimates of in-migration to
ensure that the variance in the LFS control totals did not cause unacceptable levels of
variance in the calibrated estimates. A method was developed that specifically
accounts for the extra uncertainty introduced through calibration constraints that
derive from combining survey estimates. It also accounts for the possibility of
reduced variability where calibration constraints derived from the LFS have less
variability than the IPS estimates of the same quantities.
For ten out of the twelve countries/regions the variance was smaller for the new
calibrated estimates. This results in a decrease in the coefficient of variation (CV), the
ratio of sampling variation to the estimate itself, which is used as an indicator of
robustness of the estimate. The CVs for the London and South East regions were very
low for the original IPS estimates, but were increased by a very small factor for the
calibrated estimates. Table 1 shows the coefficients of variation for the original and
calibrated estimates by constituent country of the UK and government office region in
England.

The calibration was set up in such a way as to leave estimates for age and sex
subgroups unchanged. Some changes in the variance of these estimates were evident
but were not large. For variables not accounted for by the calibration control totals
the variance is generally increased but this difference is small and is traded for the
reduction in bias. The subgroups showing a large increase in variance are cross
tabulations (such as age by citizenship) where estimates are based on small numbers.

Annex A – Calibration Methodology

Calibration is used in surveys and is a method that compensates for differences in the
characteristics in the survey and in the population. The population information is
usually available in aggregate form. The idea is to find new weights wk that are as
close as possible to the original weights ak . The new weights are subject to the

The GES (Generalised Estimation System) program in SAS implements the
generalised least squares distance function. This method gives the generalised
regression estimator (GREG) and the framework for GES is based on the theory of the
generalised regression estimator. Sometimes the least squares distance function can
give negative weights or very large weights. In this case lower and upper bounds can
be included to avoid extreme weights.

Calibration of IPS weights

GOR/country level totals and London/non-London age/sex totals were used to
calibrate the IPS weights. Proportions from the three years of averaged LFS data were
used to obtain the control totals. The averaged LFS GOR/country totals were scaled
using the overall IPS total to ensure that the total number of migrants was the same as
that given by IPS. The calibration was carried out using the GES program in SAS.
